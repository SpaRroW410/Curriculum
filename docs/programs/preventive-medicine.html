Antenatal Care: The WHO 2016 Model

🟢 MUST KNOW

In 2016, WHO increased its recommended antenatal care from 4 to a minimum of 8 contacts, scheduled at approximately 12, 20, 26, 30, 34, 36, 38, and 40 weeks of gestation — one contact in the first trimester, two in the second, and five in the third.

Purpose: provide respectful, individualized, person-centred care at every contact, combining effective clinical interventions with timely information and psychosocial/emotional support — not just a checklist of tests.

High-Risk Pregnancy

🟢 MUST KNOW

A high-risk pregnancy is one with pre-existing or current conditions that raise the risk of complications for mother, fetus, or newborn. Risk factors fall into five broad categories:

  • Demographic — age under 18 or over 35
  • Adverse pregnancy history — previous miscarriage, stillbirth, or complication
  • Medical complications — hypertension, diabetes, kidney/autoimmune/ thyroid disease
  • Pregnancy-related conditions — arising during the current pregnancy
  • Environmental/social determinants — poverty, poor nutrition, low education, unstable housing

Early identification — ideally at the first antenatal contact — allows special monitoring and referral before complications become severe.

The Under-Five Clinic

🟢 MUST KNOW

The Under-Five Clinic (adapted from the Western “well-baby clinic” concept) is a single access point delivering preventive, promotive, curative, referral, and educational services together for children under five.

The symbolic triangle used to teach this concept:

  • Apex — care during illness (curative)
  • Left side — adequate nutrition
  • Right side — immunization
  • Central inner triangle — family planning
  • Border line — health education for the mother

Essential Newborn Care and Kangaroo Mother Care

🟢 MUST KNOW

Between birth and the Under-Five Clinic’s ongoing care lies the neonatal period — the highest-risk window in childhood, and the target of the Neonatal Mortality Rate defined in the Health Information chapter.

  • Essential Newborn Care — immediate drying and warming, delayed cord clamping, early (within one hour) initiation of breastfeeding, and eye/cord care — a simple bundle proven to reduce neonatal deaths
  • Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) — continuous skin-to-skin contact between mother and newborn, combined with exclusive breastfeeding; particularly valuable for low-birth-weight and preterm infants (recall the Nutrition chapter’s coverage of low birth weight), where it improves thermoregulation, breastfeeding success, and survival at far lower cost than incubator care

Why this fits between antenatal and under-five care: it is the preventive bridge connecting the WHO 8-contact antenatal model to the Under-Five Clinic — most neonatal deaths are preventable with this simple bundle, not advanced technology.

Life-Stage Care and Levels of Prevention

🟢 MUST KNOW

Chapter 2’s levels of prevention apply directly across all three life stages in this chapter:

Level Obstetrics Paediatrics Geriatrics
Primary Folic acid supplementation pre-conception Routine immunization Fall-prevention advice
Secondary Antenatal risk screening Growth monitoring, developmental screening Screening for hypertension, diabetes, cognitive decline
Tertiary Managing a diagnosed complication Rehabilitation after childhood illness/disability Managing established chronic disease, preventing further decline

The WHO Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030)

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Proclaimed by the UN in December 2020, following WHO endorsement, with the vision that all people live longer, healthier lives. Its four action areas:

  1. Changing how we think, feel, and act toward age and ageing
  2. Developing communities that foster older people’s abilities
  3. Delivering person-centred, integrated care and primary health services responsive to older people
  4. Providing access to long-term care when needed

Explicitly aligned with the 2030 Sustainable Development Agenda’s principle of “leaving no one behind” (Chapter 12).

Immunization Schedule Principles

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Rather than memorizing a full vaccine-by-vaccine schedule, the underlying principles matter most for exams:

  • Vaccines are timed to protect infants as maternal antibody protection wanes, and before natural exposure risk rises
  • Cold chain maintenance (keeping vaccines at the correct temperature from manufacture to administration) is essential to vaccine efficacy
  • Universal Immunization Programme (UIP) is India’s platform delivering the national schedule free of cost through the general health system — the same primary-care infrastructure discussed in Chapter 11

Geriatric Preventive Screening Priorities

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Preventive care priorities shift with age — common geriatric screening targets include:

  • Hypertension, diabetes, and other NCDs (Chapter 7)
  • Falls risk — a leading cause of injury-related disability in older adults
  • Cognitive decline — early detection of dementia
  • Polypharmacy review — older adults are disproportionately affected by irrational medicine use (Chapter 8), given multiple concurrent prescriptions

Fetal Origins of Adult Disease

🟣 MAY KNOW

As introduced in the Nutrition chapter, the Barker hypothesis links poor fetal and early-childhood nutrition to higher adult NCD risk — meaning antenatal and under-five preventive care isn’t only about short-term child survival, but also shapes a person’s health trajectory decades later into adult and geriatric life.

Continuum of Care Across the Lifespan

🟣 MAY KNOW

This chapter’s three life stages are not isolated silos: RMNCH+A (Chapter 11) already frames maternal, newborn, child, and adolescent health as one continuum; the same logic extends further to encompass adult and geriatric preventive care — a person-centred health system should follow an individual across their entire life course, not treat each stage as a separate programme.
