Modes of Inheritance

🟢 MUST KNOW

Four basic single-gene inheritance patterns:

Pattern Copies needed Example
Autosomal dominant One copy, from either parent Huntington’s disease, achondroplasia, Marfan syndrome
Autosomal recessive Two copies, one from each parent Sickle cell disease (Chapter 15), cystic fibrosis, thalassemia
X-linked dominant One copy causes disease in either sex X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets
X-linked recessive One copy causes disease in males; two needed in females Haemophilia A, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, red-green colour blindness

Exam tip: males need only one copy of an X-linked recessive variant to be affected (they have only one X chromosome), which is why these conditions are more common in males than females.

Consanguinity and Genetic Disorder Risk

🟢 MUST KNOW

Consanguinity — marriage between close biological relatives — increases the chance that both parents carry the same recessive gene variant, raising the risk that their children inherit two copies and develop a recessive genetic disorder.

  • Studies from South Asia and the Middle East report up to a 2.5-fold increased risk of congenital disorders in consanguineous populations
  • In India, consanguinity and endogamy (marrying within a defined community), combined with falling infant mortality (more affected children now surviving to be counted), have contributed to a higher observed prevalence of genetic disorders

Direct link to Chapter 15: this is the same underlying mechanism behind concentrated sickle cell disease prevalence in specific tribal populations.

Genetic Counseling

🟢 MUST KNOW

Genetic counseling is a communication process providing information and support to individuals/families who have, or are at risk of, a genetic disorder — integrating family and medical history to assess recurrence risk, education about inheritance, and support for informed decision-making.

Community medicine role: beyond the individual family, genetic counseling contributes to reducing the genetic burden of disease in the community over time — a population-level goal, not just an individual clinical service, consistent with community medicine’s broader shift in unit of concern (Chapter 6) from patient to population.

Thalassemia in India

🟢 MUST KNOW

India carries a very large share of the global thalassemia burden:

  • Estimated 45 million carriers of β-thalassemia nationally
  • Approximately 15,000 infants born with homozygous β-thalassemia each year — about 10% of all thalassemia births worldwide
  • Carrier frequency varies sharply by community — from near 0% up to ~17% in specific ethnic/community groups (e.g. Sindhis, certain Punjabi and Bengali communities, some tribal populations)

Screening strategies used in India: antenatal carrier screening (most feasible in urban antenatal clinics), premarital screening, cascade screening (testing relatives of a known carrier), and newborn screening pilots (e.g. the UMMID project in Jharkhand).

The Case for Universal Screening

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Given thalassemia’s scale in India, public health literature has increasingly argued for a universal (not just targeted) thalassemia screening programme — reflecting the same population-strategy logic from Chapter 7 (Rose): a condition common enough across the general population may be better addressed by broad, systematic screening than by relying only on identifying “high-risk” families after the fact.

Genetic Counseling Awareness Gaps

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Indian studies report low awareness and utilization of genetic counseling services, even among consanguineous couples who are at objectively higher risk — a structural gap similar to the mental health treatment gap (Chapter 10) and reinforcing Chapter 17/18’s broader point that information availability alone doesn’t guarantee uptake without active outreach, cultural sensitivity, and trust-building.

Community Genetics

🟣 MAY KNOW

Community genetics is the applied field studying how genetic disorder patterns are shaped by a population’s specific marriage practices (consanguinity, endogamy) and demographic history — distinct from clinical genetics’ focus on individual patients, and closely related to the population-level epidemiological thinking introduced in Chapter 3.

Newborn Screening Beyond Thalassemia

🟣 MAY KNOW

Programmes like UMMID (Unique Methods of Management and treatment of Inherited Disorders) screen newborns for multiple inherited metabolic disorders simultaneously, not just haemoglobin disorders — reflecting the same multiphasic-screening logic introduced in the Foundations section’s Screening chapter, applied here at the genetic/metabolic level rather than to adult chronic disease.
