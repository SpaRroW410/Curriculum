The ILO/WHO Joint Definition

🟢 MUST KNOW

Adopted by the Joint ILO/WHO Committee on Occupational Health (1950), revised 1995 — occupational health aims at:

  • Promotion and maintenance of the highest degree of physical, mental, and social well-being of workers in all occupations
  • Prevention of ill-health caused by working conditions
  • Protection of workers from risks adverse to health in their employment
  • Placing and maintaining workers in a job matched to their physiological and psychological capacity
  • Adapting work to the worker, and the worker to the job — not only the other way around

Notice the parallel to Chapter 1’s holistic WHO definition of health — occupational health explicitly extends “complete physical, mental, and social well-being” into the workplace setting.

Classifying Occupational Hazards

🟢 MUST KNOW

Six recognized categories of workplace hazard:

  • Physical — noise, vibration, extreme temperature, radiation, poor lighting
  • Chemical — solvents, dusts, fumes, gases, pesticides
  • Biological — bacteria, viruses, fungi (e.g. occupational tuberculosis exposure in healthcare workers)
  • Ergonomic — poor posture, repetitive movement, awkward lifting, poor workstation design
  • Psychosocial — work-related stress, bullying, harassment, burnout
  • Safety — unsafe machinery, obstructed walkways, slippery floors (immediate injury risk, distinct from the slower-onset categories above)

Pneumoconiosis

🟢 MUST KNOW

Pneumoconiosis — a group of occupational lung diseases caused by inhalation of mineral dust, producing lung inflammation and scarring. Major forms:

  • Silicosis — from crystalline silica dust (mining, quarrying, sandblasting); nodular lung lesions
  • Asbestosis — from asbestos fibres; scarring often not symptomatic until years after initial exposure
  • Byssinosis — from cotton dust (textile industry)
  • Bagassosis — from bagasse (sugarcane fibre residue) dust

Common thread: long latency between exposure and symptom onset — directly echoing Chapter 3’s natural-history-of-disease concept, but on an occupational-exposure timescale of years to decades.

Employees’ State Insurance (ESI) Scheme

🟢 MUST KNOW

Workers registered under the Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948 receive medical treatment through ESI hospitals, providing a social insurance mechanism specifically for organized-sector workers, including coverage for occupational disease and injury.

State-level policy example: Haryana’s pneumoconiosis rehabilitation policy provides financial aid and pension support to affected workers — illustrating how national ESI coverage is complemented by state-specific disease-focused schemes for occupational conditions.

Objectives of Occupational Health Practice

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Modern occupational health practice organizes around three objectives:

  1. Maintenance and promotion of workers’ health and working capacity
  2. Improvement of the working environment and work itself, to be conducive to safety and health
  3. Development of work organizations and working cultures that support health, not just compliance with minimum safety standards

Occupational Disease and the Levels of Prevention

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Chapter 2’s levels of prevention apply directly to occupational health:

  • Primary — engineering controls (ventilation, dust suppression), personal protective equipment, pre-placement fitness assessment
  • Secondary — periodic occupational health screening (e.g. chest X-rays for dust-exposed workers) to catch pneumoconiosis before advanced symptoms
  • Tertiary — rehabilitation and compensation (like the ESI scheme) once occupational disease has developed

The Global Burden of Occupational Risk

🟣 MAY KNOW

The WHO/ILO Joint Estimates project systematically quantifies the global disease burden attributable to occupational risk factors — 19 selected risks across 183 countries — providing the evidence base for prioritizing occupational health interventions the same way Chapter 16’s DALY framework prioritizes disease burden more broadly.

From Occupational Health to “Healthy Workplaces”

🟣 MAY KNOW

Occupational health practice has evolved beyond preventing disease and injury toward a broader “healthy workplaces” framing — incorporating psychosocial well-being, work-life balance, and organizational culture as legitimate occupational health targets, not just physical hazard control — reflecting the same expansion from a purely biomedical to a holistic view of health traced across this curriculum since Chapter 1.
