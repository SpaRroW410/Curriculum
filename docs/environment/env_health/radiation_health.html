Ionizing vs. Non-Ionizing Radiation

🟢 MUST KNOW

Radiation is energy emitted as electromagnetic waves or moving subatomic particles.

Ionizing Non-ionizing
Examples X-rays, gamma rays (EM); alpha and beta particles (corpuscular) UV, visible light, infrared, microwave, radio-frequency
Mechanism Removes tightly-bound electrons, creating ions; penetrates and damages tissue Generally causes heating effects only
Danger Alpha particles: ~10× more harmful than X/gamma rays if internalized (inhaled/ingested), but poor external penetration; gamma/X-rays: short wavelength, deeply penetrating Comparatively low energy, less harmful

Sources of Radiation Exposure

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Natural (~0.1 rad/year total): cosmic rays (higher at altitude — a commercial pilot receives ~300 mrad/year), terrestrial radiation from radioactive elements in soil/rock (much higher in uranium-bearing regions, e.g. parts of Kerala), and internal radiation from radioactive material (K40, etc.) normally present in body tissue.

Man-made: medical/dental X-rays are the largest man-made source of population radiation exposure; radioactive fallout from nuclear explosions (Cs137, Sr90 — both persist for decades, half-lives of ~28–30 years); minor contributions from everyday items.

Radiation Units

🟣 MAY KNOW

Quantity Older unit SI unit
Activity (disintegrations/time) Curie (Ci) Becquerel (Bq)
Exposure (ionization in air) Roentgen Coulomb/kg
Absorbed dose (energy/mass) Rad Gray (Gy) — 1 rad = 0.01 Gy
Dose equivalent (biological effect) Rem Sievert (Sv) — 1 Sv = 100 rem

Dose equivalent (H) = Absorbed dose (D) × Quality factor (Q) — Q = 1 for X-rays/gamma rays/electrons, but Q = 20 for alpha particles, reflecting their much greater biological damage per unit of absorbed energy.

Biological Effects by Dose

🟢 MUST KNOW

Acute whole-body exposure produces a dose-dependent spectrum:

Dose (rad) Effect
<5 No observable effect
5–150 Detectable blood changes; nausea/fatigue at the higher end
150–1,100 Severe blood changes; at 300–500 rad, up to 50% mortality within 60 days without intensive care (bone marrow destruction)
1,100–2,000 Near-certain death within 1–2 weeks (GI system destruction)
>2,000 Death is certain; >5,000 rad disables CNS control of breathing/circulation

Genetic effects differ from somatic effects: somatic damage appears within the exposed person’s lifetime, while genetic effects (from chromosome/point mutations) appear in offspring.

Radiation Protection

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Permissible dose: additional man-made exposure should not exceed 5 rad/year, on top of ~0.1 rad/year natural background
  • Shielding: lead aprons (0.5 mm lead) reduce scattered X-ray intensity by over 90% for occupationally exposed workers
  • Monitoring: film badges/dosimeters track cumulative worker exposure
  • Governing bodies: the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP), IAEA, and WHO set and coordinate international dose limits and safety recommendations — this field is termed radiation hygiene

Noise: Definition and Sources

🟢 MUST KNOW

Noise is best defined as “wrong sound, in the wrong place, at the wrong time” — not simply “unwanted sound,” since that definition is too subjective. The term “noise pollution” captures its status as a modern public-health hazard.

Sources: automobiles, aircraft, industry (acute near railway junctions, traffic circles, airports); domestic sources (TV, radio); and recreational sources (loudspeakers, pressure horns).

Properties:

  • Loudness — measured in decibels (dB); normal conversation 60–65 dB, whispering 20–30 dB, boiler factories ~120 dB. Daily exposure up to 85 dB is broadly the tolerable limit for hearing
  • Frequency — measured in Hertz (Hz); the human ear detects roughly 20–20,000 Hz

Effects of Noise Exposure

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Auditory: noise-induced hearing loss, classically showing a characteristic dip at 4,000 Hz on audiogram
  • Non-auditory:
    • Interference with speech communication (300–500 Hz range most disruptive)
    • Annoyance (a psychological response, more pronounced in neurotic individuals)
    • Reduced work efficiency and concentration
    • Physiological changes — raised blood pressure, raised intracranial pressure, increased heart/breathing rate, sweating, sleep interference

Control of Noise

🟣 MAY KNOW

  • Urban planning — zoning to separate industrial/transport areas from residential; green belts between roads and housing
  • Vehicle control — restricting heavy vehicles on narrow/residential streets; prohibiting indiscriminate horn use
  • Building design — acoustic insulation, detached rather than continuous construction
  • Occupational protection — hearing protection mandatory above 85 dB, worker rotation away from noisy areas, periodic audiometry
  • Legislation and education — legal noise limits plus public awareness, since no noise-abatement programme succeeds without community participation
