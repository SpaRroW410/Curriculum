Hypertension is both a disease in its own right and the single largest modifiable risk factor for stroke, covered next — the two modules are best read together.

Definition and Burden

🟢 MUST KNOW

Hypertension is diagnosed when, on two separate days, systolic BP ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥90 mmHg.

  • Global: ~1.13 billion people affected, two-thirds in low- and middle-income countries; prevalence over 60% in people aged 60+
  • India (NFHS 2015–16): 15% of men, 11% of women hypertensive; prevalence rises consistently with BMI
  • Called the “silent killer” — most affected people have no symptoms and are unaware of their status, which is why regular BP measurement matters more than symptom-based detection

Global NCD target: reduce hypertension prevalence by 25% by 2025 (2010 baseline).

Classification

🟢 MUST KNOW

Category Systolic (mmHg) Diastolic (mmHg)
Optimal <120 <80
Normal 120–129 <85
High-normal 130–139 85–89
Grade 1 (mild) 140–159 90–99
Grade 2 (moderate–severe) ≥160 ≥100

When symptoms do occur (usually only at higher grades): early morning headache, nosebleeds, irregular heart rhythm, vision changes, tinnitus; severe hypertension can cause fatigue, nausea, confusion, and chest pain.

Essential vs. Secondary Hypertension

🟣 MAY KNOW

  • Essential (primary) hypertension (~90–95% of cases) — no single identifiable cause; arises from a combination of genetic and lifestyle factors (see risk factors below)
  • Secondary hypertension (~5–10% of cases) — a specific, identifiable underlying cause: renal disease (most common secondary cause), endocrine disorders, or coarctation of the aorta

Why this distinction matters clinically: secondary hypertension is suggested by early onset (young age), very severe or resistant BP readings, or sudden worsening of previously controlled hypertension — findings that should prompt a search for an underlying cause rather than simply escalating standard antihypertensive therapy.

Target-Organ Damage

🟢 MUST KNOW

Uncontrolled hypertension is dangerous precisely because it silently damages multiple organs over years — this is why it is called the silent killer, beyond simply being asymptomatic:

Organ Damage
Heart Left ventricular hypertrophy, coronary artery disease, heart failure
Brain Stroke (ischaemic and haemorrhagic — see next module), cognitive decline
Kidney Nephrosclerosis, progressive chronic kidney disease
Eyes Hypertensive retinopathy — visible on fundoscopy, used clinically to grade chronicity/severity
Blood vessels Accelerated atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysm

Why this table matters for prevention: it is the reason secondary prevention (early detection via BP screening) and tertiary prevention (controlling BP after diagnosis) are pursued so aggressively — the goal is not just controlling the number, but preventing this irreversible downstream organ damage.

Risk Factors and Prevention

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Risk factors: age, sex, genetic factors, ethnicity, obesity, high salt intake, alcohol use, physical inactivity, environmental stress.

Applying the three-level prevention framework (Chapter 2):

  • Primary prevention — reduce population-wide incidence through low salt diet, regular exercise, stress reduction, and weight/BMI control
  • Secondary prevention — early case detection (routine BP screening, since most cases are asymptomatic) plus antihypertensive drug therapy once diagnosed
  • Tertiary prevention — in patients with established hypertension, preventing/limiting target-organ damage through sustained BP control, and managing complications (e.g. heart failure, CKD) once they occur

Treatment approach: lifestyle modification and stress management as the foundation, with antihypertensive medication (e.g. amlodipine, telmisartan, chlorthalidone — representative drug classes, not a prescribing guide) added for confirmed hypertension.
