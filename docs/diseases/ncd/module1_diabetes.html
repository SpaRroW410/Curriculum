Definition, Types, and Burden

🟢 MUST KNOW

Diabetes mellitus is a heterogeneous syndrome of chronic hyperglycaemia from defective insulin secretion, action, or both, disturbing carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism — one of the four priority NCDs targeted globally.

  • Global prevalence: 8.5% (2014), projected to affect 6 in 10 people by 2040; nearly half of people with diabetes are unaware they have it
  • India: second-largest diabetic population worldwide (~73 million, 2017); urban prevalence rose from ~2% (1970s) to over 20% today, with rural areas fast catching up

Classification: Type 1, Type 2, and Gestational

🟢 MUST KNOW

  • Type 1 diabetes — autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells, causing absolute insulin deficiency; typically presents in childhood/ young adulthood, requires insulin from diagnosis
  • Type 2 diabetes (majority of cases) — a combination of insulin resistance and relative (not absolute) insulin deficiency; strongly linked to obesity and lifestyle factors, usually presents in adulthood but rising in younger age groups
  • Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) — glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy; a risk factor for both future maternal type 2 diabetes and macrosomic birth, which is itself listed among the screening indications below

Why this classification matters for prevention: type 1 has no established primary prevention strategy (it is autoimmune, not lifestyle-driven), whereas type 2 is where population-level primary prevention (diet, physical activity) has real impact — this module’s prevention content applies principally to type 2.

Risk Factors

🟢 MUST KNOW

Category Examples
Agent Beta-cell destruction (viral: rubella, mumps, coxsackie B4; autoimmune), genetic defects in insulin structure/function
Host Age, obesity (strongest modifiable risk factor), genetics (~90% concordance in identical twins for type 2), family history
Environmental / behavioural Physical inactivity, diet high in saturated fat and refined carbohydrate, smoking (elevated risk persists ~10 years after cessation)

Clinical features: many patients are asymptomatic; classic presentation includes polyuria, polydipsia, recurrent genital infections, and generalized pruritus — only about 1 in 5 people can recognize their own symptoms.

Screening and Diagnosis

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Whom to screen: family history of diabetes, overweight/obesity, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, sedentary lifestyle, history of gestational diabetes or macrosomic birth, cardiovascular disease.

WHO diagnostic criteria:

Normoglycaemia Prediabetes Diabetes
FPG (mg/dL) <110 110–125 (IFG) ≥126
2-hour PG (mg/dL) <140 140–199 (IGT) ≥200
HbA1c <5.7% 5.7–6.4% ≥6.5%

Re-screen normoglycaemic individuals every 3 years; prediabetic individuals annually. OGTT uses a 75 g glucose load, with fasting and 2-hour samples.

Complications

🟢 MUST KNOW

Diabetes is dangerous largely because of its complications, split into two categories with very different time courses:

  • Acute complications — diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA, mainly type 1: absolute insulin deficiency leads to fat breakdown and ketone production), hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (mainly type 2), and hypoglycaemia (usually a treatment complication rather than a disease complication itself)
  • Chronic complications, from sustained hyperglycaemia over years:
    • Microvascular — retinopathy (a leading cause of adult blindness), nephropathy (a leading cause of chronic kidney disease and dialysis need), neuropathy (loss of protective sensation, contributing to diabetic foot ulcers and amputation)
    • Macrovascular — accelerated atherosclerosis leading to coronary artery disease, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease — the same endpoints covered in the hypertension and stroke modules, since diabetes, hypertension, and stroke share overlapping vascular pathology

Why this matters for management: the goals listed below (“prevention of acute/chronic complications”) are not abstract — the specific screening a diabetic patient needs (annual fundoscopy, renal function, foot examination) is defined directly by this complication list.

Management Principles

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Goals: glycaemic control, prevention of acute/chronic complications, symptom relief, managing comorbidities (obesity, hypertension, dyslipidaemia), and behavioural change support.

Lifestyle modification (first-line, all patients): diet (complex carbohydrates and fibre over refined sugar; limit saturated/trans fat), regular physical activity, tobacco cessation, stress management, and structured diabetes education on self-care and monitoring.

Pharmacological management (broad categories, by mechanism):

  • Oral hypoglycaemic agents — sulfonylureas (stimulate insulin secretion), biguanides/metformin (reduce hepatic glucose output; first choice in obese type 2 patients), and others targeting glucose absorption or post-prandial spikes
  • Insulin — required at diagnosis for symptomatic hyperglycaemia, ketosis, severe infection, or very high FPG/HbA1c; also used in pregnancy, acute stress states, and hepatic/renal failure
