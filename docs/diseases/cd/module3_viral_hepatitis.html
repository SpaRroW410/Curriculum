Viral Hepatitis

Learning Objectives:

  • Compare hepatitis A–E by transmission route, incubation, and chronicity
  • Interpret the HBV serological marker panel (HBsAg, HBeAg, anti-HBs)
  • Describe hepatitis B prevention: vaccine, HBIG, and passive-active immunization

🟢 MUST KNOW · 🟡 SHOULD KNOW · 🟣 MAY KNOW · 🧡 QUESTION

Five clinically distinct viruses cause “viral hepatitis” — this module organizes them by the single most useful axis for undergraduate exams: faeco-oral (A, E) vs. blood-borne/parenteral (B, C, D) transmission, since that axis predicts chronicity, prevention strategy, and severity in pregnancy.

Hepatitis A–E at a Glance

🟢 MUST KNOW

Type Route Incubation Chronic state? Vaccine
A Faeco-oral 10–50 days No Yes (inactivated/live)
B Parenteral, perinatal, sexual 30–180 days Yes Yes (recombinant)
C Parenteral (blood) 2 weeks–6 months Yes (55–85%) No
D Parenteral, requires HBV co-infection Variable Yes, if HBV chronic Prevented via HBV vaccine
E Faeco-oral (water) 3–8 weeks Usually no (chronic only if immunosuppressed) Yes (China only)

Exam pattern: A and E are faeco-oral, self-limiting, and never become chronic; B, C, and D are blood-borne and can become chronic — this single distinction predicts most of what differs in prevention and prognosis below.

Hepatitis B: Serological Markers

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

HBV has three antigen–antibody systems that together stage infection — classic MBBS exam material:

  • HBsAg (surface antigen, “Australia antigen”) — presence beyond 6 months defines the chronic carrier state
  • HBeAg — marker of active viral replication and higher infectivity
  • Anti-HBs — indicates immunity, either from past infection or vaccination
  • Anti-HBc (IgM) — marks recent/acute infection

High-risk groups: surgeons (~50× general-population incidence), transfusion recipients, healthcare/lab workers, infants of HBV-carrier mothers, and people who are immunocompromised — all recommended for screening and vaccination.

Hepatitis B: Prevention

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Hepatitis B vaccine — recombinant (HBsAg-based), given IM in the deltoid (anterolateral thigh in infants); 3 doses at 0, 1, and 6 months; included in India’s pentavalent vaccine (DPT + Hep B + Hib)
  • Hepatitis B Immunoglobulin (HBIG) — passive immunization for acute exposure (needle-stick, newborn of a carrier mother); most effective within 6 hours, ideally not later than 48 hours
  • Passive-active immunization — HBIG + vaccine given together is more effective than either alone, and is the standard approach for a newborn of an HBsAg-positive mother

No specific curative treatment exists for hepatitis B — this is why prevention (vaccination, blood screening) is the primary public-health strategy.

Hepatitis C: A Changing Picture

🟣 MAY KNOW

  • Transmitted almost entirely via blood exposure (transfusion, needle-stick, injection drug use) — not through food, water, or casual contact
  • ~80% of new infections are asymptomatic; of those infected, 55–85% develop chronic infection, with 15–30% cirrhosis risk over 20 years
  • Treatment has been transformed: older interferon + ribavirin regimens (48 weeks, ~50% cure, significant side effects) have been replaced by direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) — 12-week courses that cure most patients with far fewer adverse effects
  • No vaccine exists for hepatitis C — prevention relies entirely on blood safety and injection safety

Hepatitis E: Special Risk in Pregnancy

🟣 MAY KNOW

  • A water-borne disease; genotype 1 (common in India and South Asia) causes community outbreaks, unlike genotype 3 (developed countries)
  • Usually self-limiting with complete recovery — except in pregnancy, where hepatitis E can cause fulminant hepatitis with a case fatality of up to 20% in the third trimester
  • No specific treatment; prevention relies on safe water and sanitation (the same measures already covered for cholera and typhoid)

Exam tip: hepatitis E is the one viral hepatitis where pregnancy itself is a major severity risk factor — this is the single fact most often tested.
