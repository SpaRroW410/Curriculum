Bacterial Food Poisoning: A Comparison

🟢 MUST KNOW

Agent Source Incubation Key feature
Staphylococcus aureus Milk products (preformed enterotoxin) 1–6 hours Rapid-onset vomiting, cramps
Salmonella Meat, milk, eggs 12–24 hours Fever, vomiting, diarrhoea
Bacillus cereus (emetic) Fried rice, pasta (preformed toxin) 1–6 hours Upper GI symptoms (vomiting)
Bacillus cereus (diarrhoeal) Soup, gravy, meat 12–24 hours Lower GI symptoms (diarrhoea)
Clostridium botulinum Home-canned/preserved food 18–36 hours Neurological: diplopia, dysphagia, weakness — can be fatal
Clostridium perfringens Reheated meat/poultry 6–24 hours Diarrhoea, cramps

Pattern to remember: a preformed toxin (Staph, B. cereus emetic form, botulism) gives fast onset within hours; an organism that must multiply and invade first (Salmonella, C. perfringens) takes longer.

Investigating a Food Poisoning Outbreak

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Standard steps, applying the general outbreak-investigation framework (Chapter 6) to a food-borne event:

  1. History-taking — interview everyone who shared the food: what was eaten, when, symptom onset and type, in order of occurrence
  2. Laboratory investigation — culture stool/vomit/food remnants; examine kitchen staff’s stool samples too
  3. Food-specific attack rates — calculated for each food item to identify the culprit
  4. Case-control study — used to statistically confirm the association between illness and a specific food

Prevention: meat inspection, food-handler personal hygiene (exclude those with wounds/diarrhoea from food handling), rapid cooling/cold storage, and thorough cooking (most agents killed above 60°C).

Assessing Dehydration Severity

🟢 MUST KNOW

Before treating, WHO’s three-category clinical assessment decides which treatment plan applies — this classification is what the ORS formula on the next slide is actually calculating for:

Category Key signs Treatment
No dehydration Alert, drinks normally, skin pinch goes back quickly Plan A — extra fluids and continued feeding at home
Some dehydration Restless/irritable, sunken eyes, drinks eagerly/thirsty, skin pinch goes back slowly Plan B — ORS in a health facility over 4 hours (the 75 mL/kg formula)
Severe dehydration Lethargic/unconscious, sunken eyes, drinks poorly or unable to drink, skin pinch goes back very slowly (≥2 seconds) Plan C — urgent IV fluids, ORS alone is insufficient

Why this matters: the 75 mL/kg oral formula on the next slide applies to Plan B (some dehydration) — a severely dehydrated, unconscious patient needs IV therapy first, not oral rehydration alone.

Oral Rehydration Therapy

🟢 MUST KNOW

The single most important intervention for acute watery diarrhoea:

ORS required (first 4 hours) = body weight (kg) × 75 mL/kg

  • Give as much ORS as the patient wants, monitoring for continued dehydration signs
  • Vomiting is not a reason to stop — wait 10 minutes, then resume slowly (a spoonful every 2–3 minutes)
  • Continue breastfeeding and normal feeding throughout — food should never be withheld or diluted; this is a major shift from older “starve the diarrhoea” practice

Maintenance therapy (after initial rehydration): oral fluid intake should match ongoing stool loss — roughly 10–15 mL/kg/hour for severe diarrhoea if stool volume can’t be measured directly.

Cholera vs. Shigella: Clinical Differentiation

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Feature Cholera Shigella
Stool Watery, “rice-water” Bloody/mucoid
Fever Absent Present
Vomiting Present Absent
Rectal pain (tenesmus) Absent Present

Why it matters: antibiotics are indicated only when the cause is clearly identified (shigella, typhoid, cholera) — routine antibiotic use in undifferentiated diarrhoea causes more harm than benefit. Cholera: doxycycline/tetracycline. Shigella: ciprofloxacin (often resistant to ampicillin/TMP-SMX).

Zinc Supplementation and Prevention

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Zinc — WHO/UNICEF recommend 10 mg/day (under 6 months) or 20 mg/day (over 6 months) for 10–14 days during acute diarrhoea. This reduces episode duration/severity and lowers diarrhoea incidence for the following 2–3 months.

Broader prevention (better MCH care practices):

  • Breastfeeding promotion — breast-fed children have far lower severe-diarrhoea and death risk than bottle-fed children
  • Appropriate weaning — not too early (before 6 months) or too late, with hygienically prepared, nutritious foods
  • Vitamin A supplementation — reduces child mortality by 19–54%, substantially via reduced diarrhoeal and measles deaths
  • Rotavirus vaccine — included in India’s Universal Immunization Programme, targeting the leading viral cause of severe childhood diarrhoea

Amoebiasis and Soil-Transmitted Helminths

🟣 MAY KNOW

Two further faeco-orally transmitted enteric conditions worth recognizing alongside bacterial/viral diarrhoea:

  • Amoebiasis (Entamoeba histolytica) — faeco-oral transmission via contaminated water/food; ranges from asymptomatic cyst passage to amoebic dysentery and, rarely, liver abscess. Control mirrors typhoid and cholera: safe water, sanitation, food hygiene
  • Soil-transmitted helminths (STH) — roundworm (Ascaris), hookworm, whipworm; transmitted via soil contaminated by human faeces (eggs ingested, or hookworm larvae penetrate skin). Public health control relies on periodic deworming, sanitation, and footwear use
  • Guinea worm (dracunculiasis) — transmitted via contaminated drinking water containing infected copepods; a WHO-targeted eradication disease, controlled almost entirely through safe water and case containment rather than drugs
