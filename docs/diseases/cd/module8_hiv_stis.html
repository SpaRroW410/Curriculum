HIV / AIDS and the National AIDS Control Programme

Transmission and Natural History

🟢 MUST KNOW

Agent: Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), a retrovirus that progressively destroys CD4+ T-lymphocytes, the cells central to cell-mediated immunity.

Transmission routes:

  • Sexual contact — the predominant mode globally and in India
  • Blood-borne — transfusion of unscreened blood, sharing needles (injection drug use)
  • Vertical (mother-to-child) — during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding

Not transmitted by casual contact, sharing food/utensils, mosquito bites, or airborne droplets — an important distinction for countering stigma (Chapter 23).

Natural history: acute infection → a long asymptomatic latent period (years) → progressive immune decline → AIDS, defined by a CD4 count falling below a threshold or the appearance of specific opportunistic infections/cancers.

HIV Infection vs. AIDS

🟢 MUST KNOW

  • HIV infection — presence of the virus, which may be entirely asymptomatic for years while progressively damaging the immune system
  • AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome) — the advanced stage, defined by a CD4 count below a set threshold or the occurrence of specific opportunistic infections (e.g. tuberculosis, Pneumocystis pneumonia, cryptococcal meningitis) or certain cancers that a healthy immune system would normally control

Why the distinction matters clinically: a person can be HIV-positive for years without having AIDS — antiretroviral therapy (next slide) aims precisely at preventing that progression from infection to syndrome.

Antiretroviral Therapy and PMTCT

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Antiretroviral therapy (ART) — a combination of drugs (Highly Active ART, HAART) that suppresses viral replication, preserving immune function; India provides ART free of cost through government ART centres, initiated as early as possible after diagnosis regardless of CD4 count (“test and treat”)
  • Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) — ART given to the HIV-positive mother during pregnancy and delivery, plus prophylaxis to the newborn, reduces vertical transmission risk from roughly 25–30% (untreated) to under 2%
  • Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) — ART started within 72 hours of a high-risk exposure (e.g. a needle-stick injury) can prevent infection from becoming established

Exam tip: ART does not cure HIV — it suppresses it to undetectable levels, which also dramatically reduces onward transmission risk (“treatment as prevention”).

India’s National AIDS Control Programme (NACP)

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

India’s HIV response has run through successive NACP phases since 1992, shifting emphasis over time:

NACP-I (1992) — awareness & blood safety → NACP-II — targeted interventions for high-risk groups → NACP-III — scale-up of prevention & treatment → NACP-IV/V (ongoing) — "90-90-90" and ending AIDS as a public health threat

  • Targeted interventions — focused prevention programmes for high-risk groups (sex workers, men who have sex with men, injection drug users, transgender persons), recognizing that HIV spreads unevenly across a population, not uniformly (recall Rose’s high-risk vs. population strategy, Chapter 7)
  • Integrated Counselling and Testing Centres (ICTC) — provide free, confidential HIV counselling and testing nationwide

Sexually Transmitted Infections: Syndromic Management

🟣 MAY KNOW

Beyond HIV itself, NACP also addresses other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) — since STIs and HIV share risk factors and STIs increase HIV transmission risk.

  • Syndromic management — treating an STI based on a recognizable cluster of symptoms (e.g. urethral discharge, genital ulcer) rather than waiting for laboratory confirmation, allowing immediate treatment at first contact, especially valuable where laboratory access is limited
  • Why this approach fits STI control specifically: delayed treatment risks both onward transmission and complications (infertility, increased HIV susceptibility), so syndromic management trades some diagnostic precision for speed and coverage
