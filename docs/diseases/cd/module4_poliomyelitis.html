Polio is a landmark eradication success story — India was certified polio-free in 2014 after being one of the last endemic reservoirs. This module pairs the biology with the surveillance/vaccination programme that achieved that result.

Agent, Reservoir, and Transmission

🟢 MUST KNOW

Agent: poliovirus (RNA virus), 3 serotypes — most paralytic outbreaks are type 1. Reservoir: humans only, no chronic carriers, no animal source. Most infections are subclinical — for every clinical case there may be ~1,000 subclinical infections in children, driving silent community spread.

Transmission: primarily faeco-oral (contaminated water, food, fingers, flies); droplet spread can occur during the acute phase and matters more where sanitation is already good.

Incubation period: 7–14 days (range 3–35 days). Most vulnerable age in India: 6 months–3 years.

Clinical Spectrum

🟢 MUST KNOW

Only about 1% of infections involve the CNS and cause paralysis — the rest fall into three non-paralytic categories:

Form Features
Inapparent (subclinical) No symptoms; detected only by virus isolation or rising antibody titre
Abortive polio Mild, self-limiting illness from viraemia; not clinically distinguishable without lab tests
Non-paralytic polio Neck/back stiffness and pain, synonymous with aseptic meningitis; recovery in 2–10 days
Paralytic polio Virus invades the CNS — see next slide

Paralytic Polio: Recognition

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Asymmetrical flaccid paralysis is the hallmark, preceded by fever at onset
  • Paralysis is descending (hip → distal limb), asymmetric and patchy, with proximal muscles more affected than distal
  • Progresses to its maximum in under 4 days (up to 4–7); deep tendon reflexes diminish before paralysis onset; no sensory loss
  • Bulbar/bulbospinal forms involve cranial nerves — facial asymmetry, swallowing difficulty; respiratory insufficiency is the usual cause of death

No specific treatment exists — management is supportive: good nursing care and early physiotherapy to minimize residual disability.

IPV vs. OPV

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

IPV (Salk) OPV (Sabin)
Type Inactivated, injectable Live attenuated, oral
Immunity induced Humoral only (IgG/IgM/IgA in serum) Humoral and intestinal (secretory IgA)
Herd effect None — doesn’t block gut transmission Strong — vaccine virus spreads to contacts, achieving herd immunity at ~66% coverage
Epidemic use Poor fit — needs multiple doses, injections risky during outbreaks Well suited — rapid, can interrupt transmission
Key risk None significant Rare Vaccine-Associated Paralytic Polio (VAPP), mostly linked to Sabin type 3

India’s routine schedule uses OPV (birth dose + primary series) with fractional-dose IPV (fIPV) introduced nationally after a 2016 VDPV2 detection — 2 intradermal fIPV doses achieve higher seroconversion than one full IM dose.

AFP Surveillance

🟣 MAY KNOW

Since polio itself is often silent, Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP) case reporting is the surveillance backbone — any child with sudden flaccid paralysis is investigated, whether or not polio is suspected clinically.

  • Sensitivity target: ≥1 non-polio AFP case per 100,000 children under 15 detected annually — proof the system is sensitive enough to catch a real case if one occurred
  • Two stool specimens, 24–48 hours apart, within 14 days of paralysis onset, shipped to a WHO-accredited lab within 72 hours
  • Environmental surveillance (testing sewage, e.g. in Mumbai) can detect circulating poliovirus even without a clinical AFP case

Vaccine-Derived Poliovirus and India’s Eradication

🟣 MAY KNOW

  • VDPV — the live OPV strain, in communities with low vaccination coverage, can genetically revert over prolonged circulation and cause paralysis indistinguishable from wild-type polio; classified as circulating (cVDPV), immunodeficiency-associated (iVDPV), or ambiguous (aVDPV)
  • Pulse Polio Immunization (PPI) — India’s mass campaign strategy since 1995: two immunization rounds 4–6 weeks apart, during the low-transmission season (Nov–Feb), targeting every child under 5 regardless of prior immunization status, as extra doses supplementing (not replacing) routine immunization

Outcome: India was certified polio-free on 27 March 2014. Wild poliovirus type 2 (eradicated 2015) and type 3 (eradicated 2019) are gone globally; type 1 is the only wild strain still circulating anywhere.
