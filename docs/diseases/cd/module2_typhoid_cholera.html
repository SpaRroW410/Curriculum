Typhoid Fever and Cholera

Learning Objectives:

  • Identify the agent, reservoir, and carrier states of typhoid fever
  • Describe the clinical course, complications, and diagnosis of typhoid
  • Explain the pathogenesis and clinical stages of cholera
  • Compare oral and injectable typhoid vaccines

🟢 MUST KNOW · 🟡 SHOULD KNOW · 🟣 MAY KNOW · 🧡 QUESTION

Both diseases share a faeco-oral transmission route and the same core control principles (safe water, sanitation, food hygiene) already introduced in Chapter 6 — this module focuses on what’s specific to each.

Typhoid Fever: Agent and Reservoir

🟢 MUST KNOW

Agent: Salmonella typhi (main cause); S. paratyphi A/B less frequent. Survives intracellularly; killed by drying, pasteurization, common disinfectants.

Reservoir — humans only:

  • Cases — infectious as long as bacilli appear in stool/urine
  • Carriers — temporary (incubatory/convalescent) or chronic (excretes bacilli for over a year, often from the gall bladder/biliary tract; develops in 2–5% of cases). Famous example: “Typhoid Mary,” linked to over 1,300 cases as a lifelong chronic carrier

Transmission: faeco-oral / urine-oral, via contaminated water, food, fingers, and flies. Incubation period: 10–14 days (range 3 days–3 weeks).

Typhoid Fever: Clinical Course

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Fever — classic “step-ladder” rise over 7–10 days, then plateau with a toxic, exhausted appearance
  • GI features — constipation or pea-soup (“khaki green”) diarrhoea, abdominal distension
  • Rose spots — pink, blanching papules on the trunk, appearing in week 2
  • Complications (up to 10%, mainly after 2 weeks untreated): intestinal haemorrhage (sudden temperature drop, blood in stool) and intestinal perforation (classically in week 3)

Case fatality: ~1% with early treatment vs. up to 10–20% untreated; mortality is roughly 10× higher in children under 4.

Typhoid Fever: Diagnosis and Control

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Diagnosis: blood culture is the mainstay (also bone marrow, stool). Widal test measures O and H antibodies (O appears day 6–8, H day 10–12) but has only moderate sensitivity/specificity and cross-reacts with other Salmonella — a negative Widal does not exclude typhoid.

Control of reservoir: early diagnosis, notification, isolation until stool/urine cultures are negative; chronic carriers treated with prolonged ampicillin/amoxicillin + probenecid, or cholecystectomy in refractory biliary carriage.

Vaccination: Vi polysaccharide (injectable, single dose, IM/SC, protection from day 7, revaccinate every 3 years) or Ty21a (oral, live-attenuated, 3-dose regimen on alternate days). Neither gives 100% protection but both reduce incidence and severity.

Cholera: Agent and Clinical Picture

🟢 MUST KNOW

Agent: Vibrio cholerae O1 (classical or El Tor biotype) or O139. Most infections are mild or asymptomatic; typical cases show sudden, profuse, painless, watery diarrhoea (“rice-water stools”) followed by vomiting, rapid dehydration, and muscle cramps.

Pathogenesis: the organism does not invade tissue — cholera toxin activates adenylate cyclase in gut epithelium, raising cAMP and driving massive fluid/electrolyte secretion into the lumen.

Without rapid rehydration, case fatality can reach 30–40% — this is almost entirely a disease of fluid loss, not tissue invasion.

Incubation period: a few hours to 5 days, commonly 1–2 days.

Cholera: Transmission and Diagnosis

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Transmission: faecally contaminated water (wells, ponds, rivers) and contaminated food/drink; gastric acidity is a key host defence — the vibrio is destroyed at pH ≤5, which is why achlorhydria and antacid use raise susceptibility.

Carriers: healthy persons excreting V. cholerae in stool; incubatory carriers are short-lived (short incubation period), but some convalescent/chronic carriage occurs.

Diagnosis: dark-field microscopy of fresh stool can give a rapid presumptive diagnosis (vibrios show characteristic “shooting star” motility); definitive diagnosis is stool culture on bile salt agar with slide agglutination (Inaba/Ogawa subtyping).

Cholera: Outbreak Control

🟣 MAY KNOW

Standard sequence once an outbreak is suspected (echoes Chapter 6’s general outbreak-investigation framework, applied to cholera):

  1. Verify the diagnosis (stool culture for V. cholerae O1/O139)
  2. Notify local/national health authorities
  3. Active case-finding in the community
  4. Establish accessible treatment centres — 90%+ of cases can be managed with oral rehydration alone; only severe dehydration needs IV fluids
  5. Rehydration therapy (mortality reduced to <1% with effective rehydration)
  6. Antibiotics as an adjunct only, once vomiting has stopped (fluoroquinolones, tetracycline, azithromycin)
  7. Sanitation measures — safe water, excreta disposal, food hygiene, disinfection

Mass chemoprophylaxis is not recommended — treating ~10,000 people to prevent one serious case is not cost-effective and has repeatedly failed to stop spread. Oral cholera vaccines (e.g. Dukoral) exist but supplement, not replace, water/sanitation measures.
