This chapter shifts focus from disease epidemiology to the medicines supply chain — access, rational prescribing, and quality — as a determinant of health-system performance.

What Are Essential Medicines?

🟢 MUST KNOW

WHO definition: essential medicines are those that satisfy the priority health care needs of a population.

Selection criteria:

  • Disease prevalence and public health relevance
  • Evidence of clinical efficacy and safety
  • Comparative cost and cost-effectiveness

The first WHO Model List of Essential Drugs was published in 1977, with 186 medicines, to help countries prioritize which medicines to stock, procure, and make affordable at every level of care.

India’s National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM)

🟢 MUST KNOW

India adapts the WHO model into its own National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM), maintained under the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) — India’s National Regulatory Authority.

  • First published 2011; most recently revised as NLEM 2022 (384 medicines across 27 therapeutic categories)
  • Inclusion criteria: approved/licensed in India, cost-effective, and stable under Indian storage conditions
  • Purpose: ensure accessibility of affordable, quality medicines at every level of healthcare, and to guide public procurement and price control

Rational Use of Medicines

🟢 MUST KNOW

WHO definition (1985): rational use means patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their individual requirements, for an adequate period of time, and at the lowest cost to them and their community.

The core criteria:

  • Appropriate indication
  • Appropriate medicine choice
  • Appropriate dosage
  • Correctly dispensed
  • Patient appropriately informed
  • Patient adherence

Forms of Irrational Use

🟢 MUST KNOW

Common patterns of irrational medicine use:

  • Polypharmacy — using too many medicines per patient
  • Inappropriate use of antimicrobials (wrong dose, for non-bacterial infections) — a major driver of antimicrobial resistance
  • Over-use of injections where oral formulations would work
  • Prescribing that doesn’t follow clinical guidelines
  • Inappropriate self-medication, often with prescription-only drugs
  • Non-adherence to prescribed dosing regimens

Scale of the problem: WHO estimates more than half of all medicines worldwide are prescribed, dispensed, or sold inappropriately, and half of all patients fail to take them correctly.

Substandard and Falsified Medical Products

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

In 2017, the World Health Assembly retired the term “counterfeit medicines” in favour of Substandard and Falsified (SF) medical products:

  • Substandard — authorized medical products that fail to meet quality standards or specifications
  • Falsified — products that deliberately and fraudulently misrepresent their identity, composition, or source

Why the term changed: “counterfeit” is a legal/intellectual-property concept (trademark infringement) and doesn’t necessarily indicate a health risk. The new terms reframe the issue as a public health problem, separate from patent and trademark disputes.

Generic vs. Branded Medicines

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Branded/innovator medicine — sold under a manufacturer’s trademark, often while under patent protection
  • Generic medicine — a bioequivalent copy of an approved medicine, sold under its chemical/generic name (or a different brand) once patent protection expires, typically at much lower cost

Generic substitution is one of the most effective, low-cost levers for improving access to essential medicines — most NLEM medicines are available generically.

Access to Medicines: Broader Determinants

🟣 MAY KNOW

Beyond the essential-medicines list itself, actual access depends on:

  • Rational selection and use (covered above)
  • Sustainable financing and affordable pricing
  • Reliable health and supply systems (procurement, storage, distribution — including cold-chain where needed)
  • Sound regulatory systems to keep substandard/falsified products out of the supply chain

These four pillars are the standard WHO framework for describing access to essential medicines as a health-system, not just a pharmacological, issue.

The 2017 SSFFC-to-SF Terminology Shift

🟣 MAY KNOW

Before 2017, WHO used the unwieldy acronym SSFFC (Substandard/Spurious/Falsely-labelled/Falsified/Counterfeit) medical products. This was simplified to SF (Substandard and Falsified) at the Seventieth World Health Assembly specifically to separate the public health question (is this medicine safe and effective?) from intellectual-property law (is this a trademark violation?) — two questions that had previously been conflated under one confusing label.
