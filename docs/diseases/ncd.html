What Is a Non-Communicable Disease?

🟢 MUST KNOW

An NCD is a disease that is not directly transmissible from person to person, and typically has a long duration and slow progression, resulting from a combination of genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioural factors.

The four major NCDs (responsible for the large majority of the global NCD burden):

  • Cardiovascular disease (CVD)
  • Cancer
  • Chronic respiratory disease
  • Diabetes mellitus

The Four Shared Behavioural Risk Factors

🟢 MUST KNOW

The same four modifiable behaviours drive all four major NCDs, which is why NCD prevention is tackled as one integrated agenda rather than disease-by-disease:

  • Tobacco use
  • Unhealthy diet
  • Physical inactivity
  • Harmful use of alcohol

Metabolic (Intermediate) Risk Factors

🟢 MUST KNOW

Behavioural risk factors act through measurable, intermediate biological changes:

  • Raised blood pressure
  • Overweight / obesity
  • Hyperglycaemia (raised blood glucose)
  • Hyperlipidaemia (raised blood cholesterol/lipids)

These metabolic factors are themselves the direct targets of most clinical NCD screening and management.

Global NCD Targets

🟢 MUST KNOW

  • WHO “25×25” target — a 25% relative reduction in premature mortality (ages 30–69) from the four major NCDs by 2025, compared with 2010 levels
  • SDG Target 3.4 — reduce premature NCD mortality by one-third by 2030 (relative to 2015), and promote mental health and well-being

“Premature” mortality in both targets specifically means death in the 30–69 age band — deaths that public health intervention can plausibly prevent.

Non-Modifiable Risk Factors

🟢 MUST KNOW

Alongside the four modifiable behaviours, NCD risk is also shaped by factors that cannot be changed:

  • Age — NCD risk rises with age for most conditions
  • Sex — some NCDs show marked sex differences in incidence
  • Genetics / family history — inherited susceptibility

Public health strategy focuses on the modifiable factors, but non-modifiable factors still matter for risk stratification and targeted screening.

WHO STEPS Surveillance

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

The WHO STEPwise approach to Surveillance (STEPS) is the standard method countries use to monitor NCD risk factors, in three progressively more intensive steps:

  1. Step 1 — Questionnaire: demographic data and behavioural risk factors (tobacco, alcohol, diet, physical activity)
  2. Step 2 — Physical measurements: height, weight, waist circumference, blood pressure
  3. Step 3 — Biochemical measurements: blood glucose and lipid levels

Countries can implement Step 1 alone or build up to all three, depending on resources.

Population Strategy vs. High-Risk Strategy

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Geoffrey Rose (1981) distinguished two prevention strategies:

  • High-risk strategy — identify and intervene on individuals at highest risk (e.g. treating severe hypertension)
  • Population strategy — shift the risk-factor distribution of the entire population slightly downward (e.g. a national salt-reduction policy)

Rose’s Prevention Paradox: because most cases of a common disease arise from the large number of people at moderate risk (not the small number at extreme risk), a population-wide strategy usually prevents more total disease than a high-risk strategy — even though the benefit to any one individual is small.

Double Burden of Disease

🟣 MAY KNOW

Many low- and middle-income countries, including India, now face a double burden of disease: they have not fully controlled communicable, maternal, and nutritional conditions, while NCDs are simultaneously rising — meaning health systems must manage both agendas at once, with finite resources.

Measuring NCD Burden: DALYs

🟣 MAY KNOW

The overall burden of a disease — combining both fatal and non-fatal impact — is commonly measured using Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs): years of healthy life lost to premature death plus years lived with disability. NCDs, being long-duration and disabling rather than always immediately fatal, are a major driver of global DALY burden. (Health indicators, including DALYs, are covered in more depth in the Health Information and Basic Medical Statistics chapter.)
