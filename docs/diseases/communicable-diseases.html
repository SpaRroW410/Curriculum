The Chain of Infection

🟢 MUST KNOW

Communicable disease transmission depends on six linked elements — break any one link, and transmission stops:

  1. Infectious agent — the causative organism
  2. Reservoir — where the agent lives and multiplies (human, animal, or environment)
  3. Portal of exit — how the agent leaves the reservoir (respiratory tract, gut, skin, blood)
  4. Mode of transmission — how it travels to a new host
  5. Portal of entry — how it enters the new host
  6. Susceptible host — an individual capable of being infected

Epidemic, Endemic, and Pandemic

🟢 MUST KNOW

Disease occurrence is classified by how its frequency compares to what’s normally expected in a place and population:

Term Meaning
Endemic Constant, expected presence of a disease in a given area/population, without importation
Sporadic Cases occur irregularly and infrequently, with no consistent pattern
Epidemic Cases clearly in excess of normal expectancy for that place, population, and season
Pandemic An epidemic spanning multiple countries/continents, usually affecting large numbers of people
Exotic A disease imported into an area where it does not otherwise occur

Zoonoses — infections naturally transmissible between vertebrate animals and humans — are classified by direction: anthropozoonoses (animal → human, e.g. rabies, anthrax), zooanthroponoses (human → animal, e.g. human TB in cattle), and amphixenoses (either direction).

Modes of Transmission

🟢 MUST KNOW

Direct transmission

  • Direct contact (touch, sexual contact)
  • Droplet spread (coughing, sneezing, over short distance)
  • Transplacental (mother to fetus)

Indirect transmission

  • Vehicle-borne — contaminated water, food, fomites
  • Vector-borne — via an insect/arthropod (mechanical or biological)
  • Airborne — droplet nuclei or dust suspended in air over distance

Reservoirs of Infection

🟢 MUST KNOW

  • Human reservoir — a case (symptomatic) or a carrier (asymptomatic but infectious)
  • Animal reservoir — diseases transmitted from vertebrate animals to humans are called zoonoses
  • Environmental reservoir — soil, water (e.g. tetanus spores in soil, Legionella in water systems)

Basic Reproduction Number (R₀)

🟢 MUST KNOW

R₀ is the average number of secondary infections produced by one infected person in a completely susceptible population.

  • R₀ > 1 → each case generates more than one new case → epidemic potential
  • R₀ < 1 → the outbreak is expected to die out
  • R₀ = 1 → disease remains endemic at a steady rate

Herd Immunity

🟢 MUST KNOW

Herd immunity is indirect protection of susceptible individuals when a sufficiently high proportion of a population is immune, breaking chains of transmission even for those who aren’t immune themselves.

Herd immunity threshold (HIT):

HIT = 1 − (1 / R₀)

Example: a disease with R₀ = 3 needs roughly two-thirds (66%) of the population immune to interrupt sustained transmission.

Levels of Prevention Applied to Communicable Disease

🟢 MUST KNOW

Chapter 2’s levels of prevention map directly onto infectious disease control:

Level Communicable disease example
Primordial Improving sanitation and housing before any outbreak risk exists
Primary Immunization, vector control, safe water
Secondary Case detection, contact tracing, early treatment
Tertiary Rehabilitation from disease sequelae (e.g. post-polio care)

Types of Vaccines

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Immunizing agents used for primary prevention fall into distinct categories, each with different implications for immunity and administration:

Type How it works Examples
Live attenuated Weakened live organism; usually strong, long-lasting immunity from few doses BCG, OPV, MMR
Killed / inactivated Whole organism inactivated; safer in immunocompromised hosts, but needs boosters Inactivated polio (IPV), rabies
Toxoid Inactivated bacterial toxin, not the organism itself Tetanus, diphtheria
Subunit / conjugate Only a specific antigenic component, reducing side effects Hepatitis B, Hib

Exam tip: live vaccines are generally contraindicated in pregnancy and immunocompromised individuals, since the attenuated organism can still replicate.

Isolation vs. Quarantine

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Isolation — separating a person already known to be infected (diagnosed or symptomatic) from others, until no longer contagious
  • Quarantine — separating a person who was exposed but is not yet known to be infected, for the duration of the incubation period, to see if disease develops

Both are control measures acting on different links of the chain of infection — isolation removes an active reservoir; quarantine interrupts transmission from a potential future case.

Carrier States

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

A carrier is an infected person who sheds the agent without (or before/after) showing overt disease — carriers matter because they’re often missed by clinical surveillance.

  • By type: incubatory (sheds during incubation) · convalescent (continues shedding after clinical recovery, e.g. typhoid, cholera) · healthy (shedding without ever having overt disease, e.g. poliomyelitis)
  • By duration: temporary (short-lived shedding) vs. chronic (indefinite shedding — the greater public-health threat, since the longer the carrier state, the greater the cumulative community risk)

Why carriers matter more than cases, sometimes: a diagnosed case is usually isolated; an unrecognized chronic carrier keeps transmitting silently.

Incubation Period and Related Intervals

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Incubation period — the time between exposure to an infectious agent and the first sign or symptom of disease. It depends on infective dose, portal of entry, and host susceptibility, and is used to set quarantine duration and trace likely sources.

Two related, easily confused intervals:

  • Generation time — from receipt of infection to that host’s own peak infectivity
  • Serial interval — the gap between symptom onset in a primary case and symptom onset in a secondary case it infects

Secondary Attack Rate

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Secondary Attack Rate (SAR) measures how contagious a disease is within a close-contact group (e.g. a household), following exposure to a primary case:

SAR = (New cases among contacts) / (Total susceptible contacts)

Related concept — incubation period: the time between exposure to an infectious agent and the first appearance of symptoms; it determines how long quarantine should last.

Outbreak Investigation: The 10 Steps

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

The standard CDC-style sequence for investigating a suspected outbreak:

  1. Prepare for field work
  2. Establish the existence of an outbreak
  3. Verify the diagnosis
  4. Define and identify cases
  5. Describe data by time, place, and person
  6. Develop hypotheses
  7. Evaluate hypotheses
  8. Refine hypotheses / conduct additional studies
  9. Implement control and prevention measures
  10. Communicate findings

(In practice these steps often overlap and don’t run strictly in order — control measures, for instance, are often started well before the investigation formally concludes.)

Emerging and Re-emerging Infections

🟣 MAY KNOW

  • Emerging infectious diseases — newly identified, or newly significant, infections in a population (e.g. a novel pathogen)
  • Re-emerging infectious diseases — previously controlled diseases that increase again (e.g. due to declining vaccination coverage, antimicrobial resistance, or ecological change)

Both categories are driven by similar factors: urbanization, travel, climate change, land-use change, and breakdowns in public health infrastructure.

Smallpox Eradication

🟣 MAY KNOW

Smallpox remains the only human disease ever declared eradicated (WHO, 1980), achieved through a global vaccination campaign combined with surveillance-containment (“ring vaccination” around each detected case) rather than mass vaccination alone. It remains the reference case study for what a successful global eradication program requires: an effective vaccine, no animal reservoir, and a recognizable, short-lived infectious stage.
