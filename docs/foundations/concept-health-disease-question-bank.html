Concept of Health and Disease — Question Bank

Concept of Health and Disease: Question Bank

A written companion to Chapter 2: Concept of Health and Disease for practice. Long essay, short essay, short-answer, reasoning-type, and case-scenario questions are for written practice — a model-answer key with key points is given at the end (not full essays). MCQs are followed by a lettered answer key; try them before checking.

The interactive, auto-checked versions of these MCQs are also available as Self-Check slides within the chapter deck itself.

Chapter 3: Concept of Health

Long Essay

  1. Define health and discuss the different models/concepts of health — biomedical, ecological, psychosocial, and holistic.
  2. Discuss the concept of positive health and describe the various indicators used to measure the health status of a community.

Short Essay

  1. Describe the dimensions of health.
  2. Discuss the spectrum of health and the iceberg phenomenon of disease.
  3. Describe the determinants of health, including political determinants.
  4. Discuss PQLI and HDI as composite indices of development and health.

Short Answers / Notes

  1. Write short notes on: (a) Impairment, disability, and handicap (b) Well-being vs wellness (c) Relativeness of health.

Reasoning Type

  1. Why is health considered a multidimensional and dynamic concept rather than a static, purely biomedical one?
  2. Why are objective indicators of health alone considered insufficient to capture the true health status of a population?

Case Scenario

  1. A 45-year-old man from a low-income household reports feeling constantly fatigued, though clinical investigations show no diagnosable disease. He has been unable to work regularly and feels socially isolated.
    1. Is this individual “healthy” by the biomedical model? Justify.
    2. How would the WHO’s definition of health assess this individual?
    3. Which dimension(s) of health are primarily affected?
    4. Where would you place this individual on the health–disease spectrum?
    5. Suggest measures, drawing on the determinants of health, that could improve this individual’s health status.

    1. Absence of disease alone

C. Assertion–Reason (A-R)

    1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    3. A is true, R is false
    4. A is false, R is true

  2. Assertion (A): Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) is considered one of the most sensitive indicators of a community’s health status. Reason (R): IMR reflects the combined effects of maternal health, nutrition, sanitation, and access to health services on the most vulnerable age group.

    1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    3. A is true, R is false
    4. A is false, R is true

  3. Assertion (A): The Physical Quality of Life Index (PQLI) does not include per-capita income as one of its components. Reason (R): PQLI is composed of infant mortality rate, life expectancy at age one, and literacy rate.

    1. Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
    2. Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
    3. A is true, R is false
    4. A is false, R is true

Answers

Long Essay / Short Essay / Short Answers / Reasoning — key points:

  1. Health = a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease (WHO, 1948). Models: biomedical (health = absence of disease, body as machine — narrow, ignores social/psychological factors), ecological (health = balance between man, agent, and environment), psychosocial (health shaped by psychological and social factors, not biology alone), holistic (integrates all of the above — physical, mental, social, and sometimes spiritual — as one interdependent whole; the model favoured today).
  2. Positive health = optimal functioning with reserve capacity to cope with stress, not just symptom-freedom. Indicators: mortality indicators (IMR, MMR, life expectancy), morbidity indicators (incidence/prevalence), disability rates, nutritional indicators, health-care delivery indicators, indicators of social/mental health, and composite indices (PQLI, HDI, DALY, QALY).
  3. Dimensions: physical, mental, social, and (per some frameworks) spiritual, emotional, vocational, and philosophical.
  4. Health–disease spectrum: a continuum from positive health through subclinical disease to overt disease and death, rather than a strict healthy/sick binary. Iceberg phenomenon: visible, diagnosed cases are only a small fraction of the true disease burden — most cases (subclinical, undiagnosed) lie hidden “below the waterline.”
  5. Determinants: biological/heredity, environment (physical, social, economic), lifestyle/behaviour, and health services — plus political determinants (policy choices, resource allocation, governance) increasingly recognized as shaping all the others.
  6. PQLI (Physical Quality of Life Index) = composite of infant mortality rate, life expectancy at age one, and literacy rate (excludes income). HDI (Human Development Index) = composite of life expectancy, education, and per-capita income — both used to compare development/health status across populations, though neither alone fully captures well-being.
    1. Impairment = loss/abnormality of structure or function; Disability = resulting restriction in performing an activity; Handicap = social disadvantage that follows. (b) Well-being = the subjective/objective state of doing well overall; Wellness = an active, ongoing pursuit of optimal health through deliberate choices. (c) Relativeness of health = what counts as “healthy” varies by population/context (e.g., height/build norms differ between populations without either being “unhealthy”).
  8. Health is multidimensional/dynamic because it results from the continuous interplay of biological, psychological, social, and environmental factors that change over time — a purely biomedical, static view (disease present/absent) cannot capture gradations of well-being, risk, or the influence of non-biological determinants.
  9. Objective indicators (mortality/morbidity rates) miss subjective well-being, quality of life, and functional/social dimensions of health; a population can show good objective indicators while individuals still report poor perceived health, or vice versa — hence the need for both objective and subjective/composite measures.
    1. No diagnosable disease means he would be classified “healthy” under the narrow biomedical model, which only looks for pathology. (b) The WHO definition would NOT classify him as healthy, since his social (isolation) and functional (unable to work) well-being are clearly impaired. (c) Primarily the social and mental/psychological dimensions, with physical (fatigue) also implicated. (d) He would sit toward the subclinical/at-risk end of the health–disease spectrum, short of overt disease. (e) Address social determinants (income support, employment/livelihood interventions), improve access to health services (screening for occult illness, mental-health support), and strengthen social support networks.
