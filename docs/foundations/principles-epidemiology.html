What Is Epidemiology?

🟢 MUST KNOW

John Last’s definition (1988), the standard reference definition:

“The study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.”

Three ideas packed into this definition:

  • Distribution — who gets disease, where, and when (descriptive)
  • Determinants — why they get it (analytical)
  • Application — using that knowledge to control and prevent disease

Aims of Epidemiology

🟢 MUST KNOW

  • Describe the distribution and magnitude of health problems in populations
  • Identify the etiology (causes and risk factors) of disease
  • Predict trends and the future disease burden
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of interventions, programs, and health services
  • Provide the evidence base to guide health policy and planning

Ratio, Proportion, and Rate

🟢 MUST KNOW

These three terms are frequently confused but are mathematically distinct:

Term Definition Numerator in denominator?
Ratio Relation of one quantity to another No — numerator and denominator are separate groups
Proportion A ratio where the numerator is a subset of the denominator Yes
Rate A proportion with a time dimension built in Yes, plus time

Example: “3 males per 2 females” is a ratio; “60% of the class is male” is a proportion; “12 new cases per 1,000 population per year” is a rate.

Incidence

🟢 MUST KNOW

Incidence measures how many new cases of disease occur in a population over a defined period:

Incidence Rate = (Number of new cases during a period) / (Population at risk over that period)

  • Measures risk — the probability of developing disease
  • Requires following a population forward in time
  • Best measure for studying disease causation (etiology)

Prevalence

🟢 MUST KNOW

Prevalence measures how many existing cases (old + new) there are at a given time — it does not distinguish when each case began:

Prevalence = (Number of existing cases) / (Total population)

  • Point prevalence — cases at one specific point in time
  • Period prevalence — cases at any time during a defined interval

Relationship (steady state): Prevalence ≈ Incidence Rate × Average Duration of Disease

A disease can have high prevalence but low incidence if it lasts a long time (e.g. diabetes) — prevalence and incidence answer different questions and must not be confused.

Descriptive Epidemiology: Person, Place, Time

🟢 MUST KNOW

Descriptive epidemiology characterizes who, where, and when disease occurs — the first step before asking why (analytical epidemiology):

  • Person — age, sex, occupation, socioeconomic status, ethnicity, behaviour
  • Place — urban vs. rural, national/international variation, local clustering
  • Time — secular (long-term) trends, seasonal patterns, cyclical patterns, and point-source (epidemic) patterns

Types of Epidemiological Studies (Overview)

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Three broad categories — detailed study designs are covered in Epidemiologic Methods:

  • Descriptive — describes distribution (person/place/time); generates hypotheses but cannot test them
  • Analytical — tests hypotheses by comparing exposed vs. unexposed groups (e.g. cohort, case-control studies)
  • Experimental — the investigator actively assigns exposure/ intervention (e.g. randomized controlled trials)

Uses of Epidemiology

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Assessing the community’s health status and identifying its health problems
  • Studying the natural history and prognosis of disease
  • Identifying syndromes by describing clinical patterns
  • Completing the clinical picture of chronic diseases by finding sub-clinical and mild cases
  • Searching for causes of disease by comparing rates in different population subgroups
  • Evaluating the effectiveness and efficiency of health services and interventions

Time Patterns of Disease

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Secular trend — gradual change in disease frequency over years to decades (e.g. declining infectious disease, rising NCD burden)
  • Seasonal trend — regular, predictable variation tied to season (e.g. diarrheal disease peaking in summer/monsoon)
  • Cyclical trend — periodic variation over a few years (e.g. some vaccine-preventable diseases before mass immunization)
  • Point epidemic — a sharp spike from a common source over a short period (e.g. a contaminated water source)

John Snow and the Broad Street Pump

🟣 MAY KNOW

In 1854, a severe cholera outbreak in London’s Soho district killed over 600 people in about ten days. John Snow, rejecting the then-dominant miasma (“bad air”) theory, mapped cholera deaths by address and traced them to a single water pump on Broad Street. He persuaded local authorities to remove the pump handle — after which the outbreak subsided. The contamination was later traced to a leaking sewer near the pump’s well.

Why it matters: this is widely regarded as the founding case study of modern epidemiology — using spatial/descriptive data to identify a cause and act, before germ theory or laboratory confirmation existed.

Population at Risk

🟣 MAY KNOW

The denominator used in incidence and prevalence calculations should, strictly, be the “population at risk” — people who could potentially develop the disease being studied (e.g. excluding already-immune or already-affected individuals, or using only women for cervical cancer incidence). Using the wrong denominator is a common source of error in interpreting epidemiological rates.
