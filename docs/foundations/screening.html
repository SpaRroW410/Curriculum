What Is Screening?

🟢 MUST KNOW

Screening is the presumptive identification of unrecognized disease in apparently healthy, asymptomatic individuals, using tests that can be applied rapidly and economically to a large population.

Screening is NOT diagnosis:

  • A screening test sorts people into “likely disease” vs. “unlikely disease” — it does not confirm disease
  • Everyone who screens positive must still undergo a proper diagnostic test to confirm or rule out the condition

Wilson and Jungner Criteria (1968)

🟢 MUST KNOW

WHO’s ten classic principles for deciding whether a condition is suitable for a screening program:

  1. The condition should be an important health problem
  2. There should be an accepted treatment for recognized disease
  3. Facilities for diagnosis and treatment should be available
  4. There should be a recognizable latent or early symptomatic stage
  5. There should be a suitable test for that early stage
  6. The test should be acceptable to the population
  7. The natural history of the condition should be adequately understood
  8. There should be an agreed policy on whom to treat
  9. The cost of case-finding should be economically balanced against overall medical care spending
  10. Case-finding should be a continuing process, not a one-time project

The Screening 2×2 Table

🟢 MUST KNOW

Every screening test is judged against a reference/gold-standard diagnosis:

Disease + Disease −
Test + True Positive (TP) False Positive (FP)
Test − False Negative (FN) True Negative (TN)

All four validity measures on the next slides come directly from this table.

Sensitivity and Specificity

🟢 MUST KNOW

Sensitivity — the ability to correctly identify people who have the disease:

Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)

Specificity — the ability to correctly identify people who do not have the disease:

Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)

Key property: both are properties of the test itself and do not depend on how common the disease is (prevalence) in the population tested.

Predictive Values

🟢 MUST KNOW

Positive Predictive Value (PPV) — given a positive test, the probability the person actually has disease:

PPV = TP / (TP + FP)

Negative Predictive Value (NPV) — given a negative test, the probability the person is actually disease-free:

NPV = TN / (TN + FN)

Key property: unlike sensitivity/specificity, PPV and NPV do depend on disease prevalence — the same test will have a lower PPV in a low-prevalence population, even with unchanged sensitivity/specificity.

Types of Screening

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Mass screening — offered to an entire population, regardless of individual risk (e.g. a national program)
  • Selective/targeted screening — offered only to a high-risk subgroup (e.g. screening smokers for lung disease)
  • Multiphasic screening — several screening tests applied together in one session (e.g. a health camp offering blood pressure, blood sugar, and vision checks together)
  • Opportunistic screening — offered to a person who is already seeking care for an unrelated reason

Lead Time Bias

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Lead time is the interval between detection by screening and the point when the disease would normally have been diagnosed clinically (from symptoms).

Lead time bias occurs when screening appears to prolong survival — because the clock on “survival” starts earlier — even if the person dies at exactly the same actual time as they would have without screening.

Why it matters: raw survival-time comparisons between screened and unscreened groups can be misleading unless lead time is accounted for.

Length Time Bias and Overdiagnosis

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Length time bias — screening preferentially detects slow-growing, less aggressive disease, because it is present and detectable for longer; fast-growing, aggressive disease is more likely to cause symptoms and be diagnosed clinically between screening rounds. This makes screened cases look like they have better outcomes, independent of any real benefit.

Overdiagnosis — the extreme case: screening detects a genuine abnormality that would never have caused symptoms or harm in the person’s lifetime, leading to unnecessary labeling and treatment.

Sensitivity–Specificity Trade-off

🟣 MAY KNOW

For a test with a continuous or graded result (e.g. a blood level), the cutoff chosen to define “positive” trades sensitivity against specificity: lowering the cutoff to catch more true cases (higher sensitivity) also increases false positives (lower specificity), and vice versa. This trade-off is often visualized with a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, used to choose an optimal cutoff.

Choosing Between Screening Designs

🟣 MAY KNOW

Mass screening is efficient for a common, serious, and well-understood condition (e.g. blood pressure), but wasteful and potentially harmful (overdiagnosis, false positives, anxiety) for rare conditions — reinforcing why the Wilson–Jungner criteria (importance, natural history, economic balance) matter before launching a program, not just whether a test technically exists.
