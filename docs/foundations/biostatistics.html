Types of Data

🟢 MUST KNOW

Choosing the right statistical technique starts with knowing what kind of data you have:

  • Qualitative (categorical) data — describes a quality/attribute, not a quantity
    • Nominal — unordered categories (blood group, sex, religion)
    • Ordinal — ordered categories without equal intervals (mild/ moderate/severe; socioeconomic class)
  • Quantitative (numerical) data — measured on a numeric scale
    • Discrete — whole-number counts only (number of children, number of episodes of diarrhoea)
    • Continuous — any value within a range (height, blood pressure, haemoglobin level)

Why this distinction matters first: it determines everything that follows — which measure of central tendency is meaningful, which chart type applies, and which test of significance is valid for that data type.

📚 Lecture 3 of 8 · CM6.L3

Charts and diagrams; statistical maps

Presenting Data

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Raw data must be organized before it can be interpreted — the same “choosing the right format” logic already introduced for population health data (Chapter 21), applied here to any dataset:

  • Tables — precise values, ordered rows/columns, the basis for every other presentation format
  • Bar diagram — compares categories (qualitative or discrete data); bar height/length represents frequency
  • Pie chart — shows proportions of a whole
  • Histogram — for continuous data grouped into class intervals; unlike a bar diagram, bars touch since the underlying scale is continuous
  • Line diagram — shows a trend over time (e.g. monthly disease incidence)
  • Statistical (spot) map — shows geographic distribution of a variable across areas/regions (e.g. district-wise disease incidence shaded on a map) — the same logic John Snow applied by hand-plotting cholera deaths around the Broad Street pump (Chapter 3)

Exam tip: a histogram’s bars touch; a bar diagram’s bars don’t — this single visual distinction encodes the deeper point that histograms represent a continuous scale with no natural gaps between categories.

Lecture 4: Measures of Central Tendency

📚 Lecture 4 of 8 · CM6.L4

Measures of central tendency

Measures of Central Tendency

🟢 MUST KNOW

Three ways to describe a “typical” value in a dataset, each suited to different circumstances:

  • Mean — the arithmetic average (sum of values ÷ number of values); uses every value, but is pulled toward outliers/skewed data
  • Median — the middle value when data is arranged in order; not affected by extreme outliers, making it the better choice for skewed distributions (e.g. income, hospital length of stay)
  • Mode — the most frequently occurring value; the only measure of central tendency usable for nominal (categorical) data

Choosing between them: symmetric, non-skewed continuous data → mean; skewed continuous data or data with outliers → median; categorical data → mode.

Lecture 5: Dispersion and Normal Distribution

📚 Lecture 5 of 8 · CM6.L5

Measures of dispersion; normal distribution

Measures of Dispersion

🟢 MUST KNOW

Central tendency alone can be misleading — two datasets can share the same mean while looking very different in spread:

  • Range — highest value minus lowest value; simplest measure, but entirely determined by the two most extreme values
  • Standard deviation (SD) — the average distance of each value from the mean; the most widely used measure of spread for data that is roughly normally distributed
  • Variance — the square of the SD; used mathematically (e.g. in further statistical tests) more often than reported directly
  • Coefficient of variation (CV) — SD expressed as a percentage of the mean (CV = (SD / Mean) × 100); allows comparing the relative spread of two datasets measured in different units (e.g. comparing variability in height vs. weight)

Why CV matters specifically: SD alone can’t compare variability across different scales — a SD of 5 kg and a SD of 5 cm aren’t comparable, but their CVs are.

The Normal Distribution

🟢 MUST KNOW

Many biological measurements (height, blood pressure, haemoglobin) approximate a normal (Gaussian) distribution — a symmetric, bell-shaped curve with defining properties:

  • Mean = median = mode, all at the centre of the curve
  • Symmetric about the mean — the two tails mirror each other
  • 68–95–99.7 rule: approximately 68% of values fall within ±1 SD of the mean, 95% within ±2 SD, and 99.7% within ±3 SD

Why this matters practically: it’s what makes the SD interpretable — knowing the mean and SD of a normally distributed variable tells you, without seeing the raw data, roughly what range most values fall in, and what counts as an unusually extreme value.

Lecture 6: Sampling Techniques and Sample Size

📚 Lecture 6 of 8 · CM6.L6

Sampling techniques and basic concepts of sample size

Sampling Techniques

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Studying an entire population is rarely feasible — a sample is selected to represent it, using one of two broad approaches:

  • Probability sampling (every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection — allows valid generalization to the population):
    • Simple random sampling — every unit has an equal chance, selected via random numbers
    • Systematic sampling — every kth unit selected from a list
    • Stratified sampling — population divided into homogeneous strata (e.g. by age group) first, then randomly sampled within each
    • Cluster sampling — naturally occurring groups (e.g. villages, schools) are randomly selected, and every unit within a chosen cluster is included
  • Non-probability sampling (selection not random — convenient but cannot support valid generalization): convenience, purposive, and quota sampling

Why the distinction matters: only probability sampling provides a statistical basis for generalizing findings from the sample back to the population — the entire logic of inferential statistics that follows depends on it.

Sample Size

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

An appropriately calculated sample size balances two competing risks: too small a sample risks missing a real effect (inadequate statistical power); too large wastes time, cost, and participant burden without added benefit.

Key factors that increase the required sample size:

  • Higher desired confidence level (e.g. 99% vs. 95%)
  • Smaller expected effect size (a subtle difference needs more participants to detect reliably than an obvious one)
  • Greater variability in the outcome being measured
  • Higher desired statistical power (the ability to detect a true effect if one exists)

Why this belongs in a curriculum, not just a statistics course: an underpowered study (Chapter 4) can fail to detect a real association purely because it enrolled too few participants — not because no association exists.

Lecture 7: Tests of Significance I

📚 Lecture 7 of 8 · CM6.L7

Tests of significance I - standard error, z test and t test

Standard Error and Confidence Intervals

🟢 MUST KNOW

  • Standard Error (SE) — the standard deviation of the sample mean itself, estimating how much a sample mean would vary if the study were repeated; SE = SD / √n — SE shrinks as sample size grows, unlike SD, which describes spread within a single sample and doesn’t shrink with larger n
  • Confidence interval (CI) — a range, calculated from the sample mean ± a multiple of the SE, that is expected to contain the true population value with a stated level of confidence (typically 95%)

Why SE matters for tests of significance: every test on the following slides ultimately compares an observed difference to the amount of variation expected by chance alone — and that expected variation is expressed using the standard error.

Tests of Significance: The t-test

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

The t-test compares the means of continuous data between two groups (or a sample mean against a known value), asking: is the observed difference larger than what chance alone would produce?

  • Unpaired (independent) t-test — compares means between two separate, independent groups (e.g. mean blood pressure in a treated vs. untreated group)
  • Paired t-test — compares means from the same subjects measured twice (e.g. before and after an intervention), accounting for the natural correlation between paired measurements

p-value: the probability of observing a difference this large (or larger) if there were truly no difference in the population; by convention, p < 0.05 is considered statistically significant — but statistical significance is not the same as clinical importance.

Lecture 8: Tests of Significance II

📚 Lecture 8 of 8 · CM6.L8

Tests of significance II - chi-square test, correlation and regression

Tests of Significance: Chi-Square Test

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

The chi-square (χ²) test is used for categorical data, testing whether the observed distribution across categories differs from what would be expected by chance — e.g. is disease frequency associated with exposure status, when both are categorical (present/absent)?

χ² = Σ [(Observed − Expected)² / Expected]

Why it’s the categorical counterpart to the t-test: the t-test needs continuous data with means to compare; chi-square instead compares counts/frequencies across categories — the correct tool once data is nominal or ordinal rather than a measurable numeric scale.

Correlation and Regression

🟣 MAY KNOW

  • Correlation — measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two continuous variables, expressed as the correlation coefficient (r, ranging from −1 to +1); r close to 0 means little or no linear relationship
  • Regression — goes further than correlation by modelling how one variable predicts or changes with another, producing an equation that can estimate one variable’s value from the other

Exam tip, revisited from Chapter 4: correlation and regression both describe association, not causation — the same causal-inference caution already introduced in the Bradford Hill criteria (Chapter 2) applies directly here.
