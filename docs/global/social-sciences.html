This chapter gives the sociological and anthropological theory underlying why health education (Ch17) and prevention programmes don’t work the same way for every population — behaviour is socially and culturally situated, not just individually rational.

The Sick Role (Talcott Parsons)

🟢 MUST KNOW

Parsons (1950s) described illness as a state of “sanctioned deviance” — because a sick person isn’t a fully productive member of society, society grants them a recognized sick role, with defined rights and obligations:

Rights:

  • Exemption from normal social responsibilities (work, school)
  • Not being blamed for their condition

Obligations:

  • To see being sick as undesirable, and want to get well
  • To seek technically competent help (a doctor) once illness persists
  • To cooperate with treatment/medical advice

Why it matters: it frames illness as a social role, not just a biological state — connecting directly back to Chapter 2’s distinction between disease, illness, and sickness.

Illness Behaviour

🟢 MUST KNOW

Health-seeking behaviour — any activity undertaken by a person who perceives themselves to have a health problem, aimed at finding an appropriate remedy. It has five recognized components:

  1. Symptom definition — recognizing something is wrong
  2. Illness-related shifts in role behaviour — adjusting daily activities in response
  3. Lay consultation and referral — discussing symptoms with family/ community before (or instead of) a formal provider
  4. Treatment actions — choosing a source of care (biomedical, traditional, or both)
  5. Adherence — following through with the chosen treatment

Key insight: none of these steps are purely medical decisions — each is shaped by social and cultural context.

Culture and Health-Seeking Behaviour

🟢 MUST KNOW

How people define illness, interpret symptoms, and decide where to seek care is shaped by cultural belief systems — including:

  • Spiritual/supernatural explanatory models of illness causation
  • Trust (or distrust) in the indigenous/traditional medical system versus the formal biomedical system
  • Perceived quality of communication with health professionals
  • Social norms around who is expected to seek care, and when

Direct link to Chapter 22: this is exactly why pure IEC-style messaging often fails — a message that ignores the target population’s explanatory model of illness is unlikely to change behaviour, however factually accurate it is.

WHO Social Determinants of Health Framework

🟢 MUST KNOW

WHO defines social determinants of health as “the circumstances in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, and the wider set of forces and systems shaping the conditions of daily life.”

The framework distinguishes two levels:

  • Structural determinants — the socioeconomic/political context that distributes power and resources unequally (governance, economic policy, education systems, social class, gender, ethnicity)
  • Intermediary determinants — the more immediate, everyday living/ working conditions this produces (employment conditions, housing, material circumstances)

Guiding principle: health equity — the absence of unfair and avoidable health differences between groups — directly extending Chapter 7’s discussion of the social gradient of health.

Medical Sociology vs. Medical Anthropology

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • Medical sociology — studies how social structures, institutions, and roles (like the sick role) shape health, illness, and the practice of medicine within a society
  • Medical anthropology — studies how culture — beliefs, meanings, and practices — shapes how illness is experienced, explained, and treated across different societies

Both disciplines inform community medicine’s core premise: health cannot be understood purely biomedically, echoing the WHO definition of health from Chapter 1.

Explanatory Models of Illness

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Every patient (and every provider) carries an explanatory model — a set of beliefs about what caused their illness, why it started when it did, what it does to the body, and what treatment should look like. Mismatched explanatory models between patient and provider — not lack of information — are a common, underappreciated cause of poor treatment adherence and dissatisfaction with care.

Stigma Revisited Through a Sociological Lens

🟣 MAY KNOW

Chapter 10 introduced stigma as a barrier to mental healthcare; medical sociology frames stigma more generally as a social process of labeling and devaluation applied to certain illnesses or conditions — applicable well beyond mental health, to conditions like leprosy, HIV/ AIDS, and tuberculosis, where the social consequences of a diagnosis can outweigh its clinical severity in shaping a person’s behaviour.

Community-Based Participatory Approaches

🟣 MAY KNOW

Recognizing that social and cultural context shapes health behaviour has practically influenced programme design — favouring approaches that involve the community itself in defining problems and designing interventions (echoing Alma-Ata’s community-participation principle from Chapter 1), rather than externally designed programmes imposed without local input.
