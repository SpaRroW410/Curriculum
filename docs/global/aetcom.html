AETCOM is a mandatory competency thread in the Indian MBBS curriculum, distinct from disease-specific chapters — it addresses how a doctor practises, not just what a doctor knows. This chapter closes the main curriculum before the closing Health Talks/Community IEC chapter.

Effective Communication in Healthcare

🟢 MUST KNOW

Communication is not incidental to clinical care — it is directly tied to diagnostic accuracy, treatment adherence, and patient satisfaction.

  • Patient-centred communication — understanding and addressing the patient’s concerns, values, and preferences, not just transmitting clinical information
  • Verbal communication — clarity, appropriate language (avoiding unexplained jargon), tone
  • Non-verbal communication — eye contact, posture, facial expression, physical space — often communicates more than words, and can contradict them if inconsistent
  • Active listening — fully attending to what a patient says (and doesn’t say) before responding, rather than formulating a reply while they’re still speaking

Why it matters: poor communication is one of the most common sources of patient complaints and diagnostic error — not lack of medical knowledge.

The Four Principles of Biomedical Ethics

🟢 MUST KNOW

The standard framework (Beauchamp and Childress) underlying essentially all modern clinical and research ethics:

Principle Meaning
Autonomy Respect the patient’s right to make informed decisions about their own care
Beneficence Act to benefit the patient — maximize good outcomes
Non-maleficence “First, do no harm” — avoid causing harm
Justice Treat people fairly; distribute benefits/burdens of care and research equitably

When principles conflict (e.g. a competent patient autonomously refuses a beneficial treatment): there is no fixed hierarchy — this is precisely what makes ethics reasoning, not a lookup table, and is why a structured decision framework (later in this chapter) matters more than memorizing the four words.

Informed Consent

🟢 MUST KNOW

The practical expression of autonomy in clinical and research practice. Valid informed consent requires:

  • Mental capacity — the ability to understand and process the information given
  • Disclosure — adequate information about the procedure, risks, benefits, and alternatives (including the alternative of no treatment)
  • Voluntariness — freedom from coercion or undue influence, including from family members or the treating team itself
  • Comprehension — confirming the patient actually understood, not merely that information was provided

Exam tip: consent obtained without genuine voluntariness (e.g. a patient agreeing only because a powerful relative is present and pressuring them) is not valid consent, even if a form is signed.

Milestones in Medical Ethics

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Modern research ethics exists because of specific historical abuses — this timeline is worth knowing as cause-and-effect, not just dates:

Nuremberg Code (1947, following Nazi medical experiments) → Declaration of Helsinki (1964) → US Public Health Service Syphilis Study at Tuskegee exposed (1972) → Belmont Report (1979) → International ethical guidelines (1980s–2000s)

The Tuskegee Syphilis Study (1932–1972): researchers observed the natural progression of untreated syphilis in African American men, deliberately withholding effective treatment (including penicillin, proven safe and effective by the 1940s) without informing participants — leading to preventable deaths and disability, and directly triggering the Belmont Report’s three core principles (respect for persons, beneficence, justice).

Nuremberg Code’s core requirements: informed consent is essential; research must be justified by anticipated benefit exceeding risk; avoidable suffering and death/disability risk must not be imposed on subjects.

A Framework for Ethical Decision-Making

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

A structured sequence for working through a genuine ethical dilemma, rather than defaulting to intuition alone:

  1. Identify the dilemma — which principles are actually in tension?
  2. Gather relevant data — clinical facts, patient values, family/ social context
  3. Evaluate options and consequences — for the patient, for others affected, for the health system
  4. Consult ethical guidelines and, where needed, colleagues/ethics committees — a dilemma faced alone is more likely to be resolved poorly
  5. Communicate transparently — with the patient/family and the care team, about the reasoning, not just the decision

Applies equally to individual clinical dilemmas (e.g. a diagnostically uncertain case, a resource-allocation decision) and to public health ethics (e.g. balancing individual liberty against population benefit in an outbreak response).

Disclosure of Medical Errors

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Medical errors — diagnostic, procedural, communication, or systemic — occur in every health system; the ethical question is not whether they occur but how they are handled afterward.

  • Why disclosure matters: builds patient trust, upholds honesty and respect for autonomy, and is the only route to systemic learning and improvement
  • Disclosure is not contingent on harm — even a harmless error is worth disclosing, since the ethical basis is honesty and transparency, not damage control
  • Common barriers: fear of litigation or blame, inadequate communication training, and an institutional culture of silence around error — all of which disclosure policies and a “just culture” approach aim to counter

Ethical Theories: A Brief Map

🟣 MAY KNOW

The four-principles framework is not the only lens available — several underlying ethical theories inform it:

  • Deontology — actions are right or wrong based on rules/duties, regardless of consequences (e.g. “never lie to a patient”)
  • Consequentialism — actions are judged by their outcomes (e.g. public health decisions weighing aggregate population benefit)
  • Virtue ethics — focuses on the character of the practitioner (honesty, compassion, courage) rather than rules or outcomes alone
  • Principlism — the four-principles approach itself, treated as a practical middle ground between these more abstract theories

Doctors as Social Agents

🟣 MAY KNOW

Professionalism extends the doctor-patient relationship outward to society:

  • Professional boundaries — maintaining appropriate limits in the doctor-patient relationship even under social or emotional pressure
  • Physician and society — doctors hold a position of public trust and accountability that extends beyond individual patient encounters to community and policy roles
  • Physicians and colleagues — collegial relationships (referral, consultation, peer review) carry their own ethical obligations of honesty and respect, distinct from the doctor-patient relationship

Trust and accountability are the throughline connecting individual ethics (informed consent), institutional ethics (error disclosure), and professional ethics (social role) — this chapter’s three threads are ultimately one continuous obligation, not three separate topics.
