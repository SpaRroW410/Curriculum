The Health Planning Cycle

🟢 MUST KNOW

A standard sequence used to plan any health programme or policy:

  1. Situation analysis — collect and interpret data on the population, morbidity/mortality, disease distribution, existing facilities and manpower, and community attitudes and beliefs
  2. Establishing objectives and goals
  3. Assessing available resources
  4. Fixing priorities
  5. Writing up the formulated plan
  6. Programming and implementation — translating policy into annual operational plans
  7. Monitoring — ongoing tracking, enabling timely correction
  8. Evaluation — assessing whether objectives were achieved (efficiency, acceptance, adequacy)

Note the direct parallel to Chapter 3’s descriptive epidemiology — situation analysis is essentially applying the person/place/time framework at the planning stage.

Setting Health Priorities

🟢 MUST KNOW

With finite resources, not every identified problem can be addressed at once. Standard criteria for prioritization include:

  • Burden of disease (DALYs — Chapter 21)
  • Equity — impact on the most disadvantaged groups (Chapter 23’s social determinants lens)
  • Feasibility — technical and political practicality of intervening
  • Cost-effectiveness — health gain per unit of resource spent

This is the planning-level application of Rose’s population vs. high-risk strategy trade-off from Chapter 7 — priority-setting decides which problems get a population-wide response at all.

India’s Five-Year Plans and Health

🟢 MUST KNOW

India ran twelve Five-Year Plans (1951–2017), formulated by the Planning Commission (established 1950, chaired by the Prime Minister), each setting specific economic and social development targets — including health-sector allocations for infrastructure, disease control, and human resources.

  • 1969 — the Gadgil formula introduced transparent, criteria- based resource allocation to states
  • 2015 — the Planning Commission was replaced by NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India), which since the 12th Plan’s completion (2017) has taken over health-sector planning and programming with a more flexible, non-five-year framework

Monitoring vs. Evaluation

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

The planning cycle’s last two steps are often confused, but ask different questions and happen on different timescales:

Monitoring Evaluation
Question asked “Are we doing what we planned?” “Did it work?”
Timing Continuous, throughout implementation Periodic — mid-term or at completion
Purpose Track process indicators, enable timely correction Judge outcomes against objectives (efficiency, acceptance, adequacy)
Example Tracking monthly ASHA home-visit counts against target Assessing whether IMR actually fell in the target districts

Why the distinction matters: a programme can pass every monitoring checkpoint (activities completed on schedule) and still fail evaluation (no measurable health outcome improved) — process fidelity is not the same as impact.

POSDCORB: Core Management Functions

🟢 MUST KNOW

Luther Gulick’s classic 1937 framework, describing the seven universal functions of any executive/manager — directly applicable to running a health programme or facility:

  • Planning
  • Organizing
  • Staffing
  • Directing
  • COordinating
  • Reporting
  • Budgeting

Why it’s still taught: it gives a simple, complete checklist for “what does managing a health programme actually involve” beyond just clinical or technical content — the same operational lens this chapter applies to Chapters 11–15’s programme content.

Applying the Cycle: A Worked Example

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Consider NSCAEM (Chapter 15): situation analysis established the disproportionate tribal-population sickle cell burden; priority setting targeted it specifically because of its concentrated, genetic, identifiable nature; implementation rolled out mass screening; monitoring tracks screening numbers against the 7-crore target; final evaluation will assess progress toward the 2047 elimination goal. Every named national programme in this curriculum has moved through these same planning-cycle stages, whether or not it’s stated explicitly.

Health Systems Building Blocks

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

WHO describes six interacting “building blocks” that any functioning health system’s planning must account for: service delivery, health workforce, health information systems, access to essential medicines, financing, and leadership/governance. Weakness in any one block — e.g. a health workforce shortage in tribal areas (Chapter 15) — limits the effectiveness of planning and management improvements made elsewhere.

From Five-Year Plans to Flexible Governance

🟣 MAY KNOW

The shift from fixed Five-Year Plans to NITI Aayog’s more continuous, flexible planning approach reflects a broader governance trend also seen in global health policy — the same broad movement from the MDGs’ fixed, time-bound targets to the SDGs’ more integrated, continuously monitored agenda (Chapter 12).

Management Span of Control

🟣 MAY KNOW

Gulick and Urwick, alongside POSDCORB, also argued for a limited “span of control” — recommending roughly 3–6 subordinates per manager for effective coordination — a principle still referenced in designing supervisory structures for large-scale health programmes with many frontline workers (e.g. ASHA supervision structures under NHM).
