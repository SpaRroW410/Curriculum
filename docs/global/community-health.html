Levels of Health Care: Not Levels of Prevention

🟢 MUST KNOW

Important distinction: this chapter’s “primary/secondary/tertiary” refers to facility/referral tiers — how care is organized and escalated — which is a different concept from Chapter 2’s levels of prevention (primordial/primary/secondary/tertiary prevention), even though the words “primary,” “secondary,” and “tertiary” are shared. Don’t conflate the two in exams.

  • Primary care — first point of contact; routine checkups, common illness management, and preventive care
  • Secondary care — specialist services, usually hospital/clinic- based, bridging primary and highly specialized care
  • Tertiary care — highly specialized, advanced diagnostics/procedures at state-of-the-art facilities, typically reached only by referral

The Referral System

🟢 MUST KNOW

Care tiers are interlinked through referral: a patient typically enters at the primary level, and is referred upward only if their condition requires the more specialized (and resource-intensive) capabilities of secondary or tertiary facilities.

Why this matters for health systems design: a functioning referral system prevents both under-treatment (a serious case stuck at a facility that can’t manage it) and system overload (routine cases flooding scarce tertiary capacity that should be reserved for complex cases).

India’s Three-Tier Rural Health Infrastructure

🟢 MUST KNOW

India’s public rural primary health care system has three pillars, with defined population norms:

Tier Population norm (general areas) Population norm (tribal/hilly/difficult areas) Role
Sub-Centre (SC) 1 per 5,000 1 per 3,000 First contact point
Primary Health Centre (PHC) 1 per 30,000 1 per 20,000 Referral unit for 6 Sub-Centres
Community Health Centre (CHC) 1 per 1,20,000 1 per 80,000 Referral unit for 4 PHCs

Note the deliberately lower population norms in tribal/hilly/difficult areas — a direct structural response to the geographic isolation challenge raised in Chapter 15’s tribal health discussion, even though Chapter 15 also documented that actual facility numbers often fall short of these norms.

Sub-Centres: The First Contact Point

🟢 MUST KNOW

The Sub-Centre is the most peripheral point of contact between the formal health system and the community, staffed to deliver maternal and child health, family welfare, nutrition, immunization, and diarrhoeal/ communicable disease control services — and to carry out interpersonal communication for behaviour change (Chapter 22’s IEC/BCC in practice).

PHC — a 4–6 bed referral unit for 6 Sub-Centres, staffed by a Medical Officer and paramedical staff.

CHC — a 30-bed referral unit for 4 PHCs, offering specialist services beyond what a PHC can provide.

Community Participation, Revisited

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

This three-tier structure is where the community-level actors named in earlier chapters actually operate:

  • ASHA workers (Chapter 11) — the community-level link feeding into the Sub-Centre
  • Village health committees (Chapter 11) — give communities a voice in how local facilities are used and monitored
  • Anganwadi Centres (Chapter 9) — often co-located with or closely linked to Sub-Centre catchment areas for nutrition/child health service delivery

The throughline: Alma-Ata’s community-participation principle (Chapter 1) is operationalized concretely through this specific tiered structure, not just as an abstract policy goal.

Underutilization and Bypassing

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

A well-documented challenge in India’s tiered system is bypassing — patients skipping the primary tier and going directly to secondary or tertiary facilities (public or private), even for conditions a PHC could manage. This undermines the referral system’s efficiency logic and contributes to overcrowding at higher tiers, often driven by perceived (or actual) quality gaps at the primary level — connecting back to Chapter 23’s discussion of trust in health-seeking behaviour.

Urban Parallel: NUHM

🟣 MAY KNOW

The Sub-Centre/PHC/CHC structure described here is specifically the rural tier system. The National Urban Health Mission (Chapter 11) adapts an analogous but distinct tiered structure for urban and peri-urban poor populations, recognizing that urban health-access barriers (informal settlements, migrant populations, different population density) differ from rural ones even where similar infrastructure logic applies.

Quaternary Care

🟣 MAY KNOW

Some health systems recognize a fourth tier, “quaternary care” — an extension of tertiary care involving highly specialized and experimental procedures (e.g. rare transplant surgery, experimental cancer treatment), usually only available at a small number of specialized national or international centres. It doesn’t have a formal, separate tier in India’s public rural health infrastructure, but is relevant terminology in broader health systems study.
