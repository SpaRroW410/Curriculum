Census, Civil Registration, and Sample Registration

🟢 MUST KNOW

  • Census — a complete, periodic (every 10 years in India) headcount with legal backing, collecting population size, composition, density, literacy, and occupational structure. Records either de facto (location at the time of enumeration) or de jure (usual place of residence)
  • Civil Registration System (CRS) — aims at universal, legal registration of every individual birth and death, for documentary and legal purposes
  • Sample Registration System (SRS), India — a sample-based statistical exercise (not universal registration), designed to estimate rates, not register individuals; operates via dual recording (continuous local enumeration plus independent retrospective half-yearly surveys) across a representative sample of units nationwide

Why SRS exists alongside CRS: where civil registration completeness is incomplete, SRS provides India’s most reliable source of birth/death rate estimates — it has served as the standard data source for India’s mortality indicators since the late 1960s.

Crude Birth Rate and Crude Death Rate

🟢 MUST KNOW

The most basic vital statistics rates, both drawn from the CRS/SRS sources above, and the starting point before the more specific mortality indicators that follow:

Crude Birth Rate (CBR) = (Live births in a year / Mid-year population) × 1,000

Crude Death Rate (CDR) = (Deaths in a year / Mid-year population) × 1,000

Why “crude”: both rates use the total population as the denominator, without adjusting for age structure — a population with more elderly people will show a higher CDR even with identical age-specific mortality, which is exactly why more specific indicators (IMR, MMR, age-specific rates) are needed alongside these crude measures, not instead of them.

Life Expectancy at Birth

🟢 MUST KNOW

Life expectancy at birth — the average number of years a newborn is expected to live, if current age-specific mortality patterns remain constant throughout their lifetime.

It is a summary indicator of mortality across every age group at once, which is why it’s widely used to compare overall health/longevity between regions or countries, rather than relying on any single age-specific death rate alone.

Infant and Maternal Mortality Indicators

🟢 MUST KNOW

Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) — deaths of infants under 1 year of age, per 1,000 live births, in a given year.

Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) — deaths of women from pregnancy- related causes, per 100,000 live births (note the different denominator scale from IMR).

Both are core indicators already referenced as SDG 3 targets (Chapter 12) — this chapter defines the measures themselves, which those targets are expressed in.

Related neonatal/perinatal measures, all per 1,000 live births unless noted:

  • Neonatal Mortality Rate (NMR) — deaths in the first 28 days of life
  • Early Neonatal Mortality Rate — deaths in the first 7 days of life
  • Perinatal Mortality Rate — (late fetal deaths ≥28 weeks gestation + deaths in the first 7 days) ÷ (total live births + late fetal deaths ≥28 weeks)

The Three Delays Model

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

A widely used framework for understanding why maternal deaths happen, not just measuring how many — most maternal deaths trace to one or more of three delays:

  1. Delay in deciding to seek care — lack of awareness of danger signs, cultural beliefs, financial constraints
  2. Delay in reaching care — transportation barriers, distance to the nearest facility
  3. Delay in receiving adequate care once at a facility — inadequate staffing/training, lack of emergency obstetric care (EmOC), blood/ drug shortages

Why this framework matters: it reframes maternal mortality reduction from “improve hospitals” alone to a systems problem — interventions targeting only Delay 3 (better facilities) fail if Delays 1 and 2 still prevent women from reaching them in time.

DALY: Breaking Down the Formula

🟢 MUST KNOW

DALY = YLL + YLD

  • YLL (Years of Life Lost) = number of deaths × standard life expectancy at age of death — the fatal component
  • YLD (Years Lived with Disability) = number of new cases × disability weight × average duration of disability — the non-fatal component

Disability weight ranges from 0 (no limitation) to 1 (a health state equivalent to death), assigned per condition based on its severity.

One DALY = one lost year of healthy life — combining death and disability into a single comparable unit, which is exactly what allowed Chapters 7 and 10 to compare the burden of NCDs and mental disorders on the same scale as fatal disease.

DALY vs. QALY

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

  • DALY — measures healthy years lost (burden); uses standardized disability weights; the dominant metric in global/population health burden studies (e.g. WHO’s Global Burden of Disease)
  • QALY — measures years lived in good health gained; uses preference-based weights (often elicited from patients or the public); the dominant metric in health economics and cost-effectiveness analysis for individual treatments

Exam tip: DALY counts what’s lost; QALY counts what’s gained — they are conceptually mirror images, built for different purposes (population burden vs. treatment cost-effectiveness).

Presenting Health Data

🟡 SHOULD KNOW

Common formats for presenting health information, each suited to a different purpose:

  • Tables — precise values, good for reference and detailed comparison
  • Bar/line graphs — trends over time or comparison across categories or places (recall Chapter 3’s person/place/time descriptive framework)
  • Pie charts — proportions of a whole (e.g. cause-of-death distribution)
  • Maps — geographic distribution — the same logic John Snow used with cholera cases in 1854 (Chapter 3)

Choosing the right format is itself a communication decision, not just a technical one — it shapes how policymakers and the public interpret the same underlying data.

National Family Health Survey (NFHS)

🟣 MAY KNOW

Alongside the Census and SRS, India’s National Family Health Survey is a large-scale household survey providing detailed data on fertility, family planning, maternal and child health, nutrition, and mortality — often used alongside SRS data to produce more granular, sub-national estimates (e.g. state- or district-level life expectancy), where SRS sample sizes alone may be too small for reliable local estimates.

Health Management Information System (HMIS)

🟣 MAY KNOW

Beyond periodic surveys and registration systems, India’s routine facility-level reporting flows into a Health Management Information System (HMIS) — data reported upward from Sub-Centres, PHCs, and CHCs (the same three-tier structure referenced in later chapters), used for ongoing programme monitoring rather than the periodic, sample-based estimates that SRS and NFHS provide.
